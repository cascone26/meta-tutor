## 2. Findings — candidate chapters

### Chapter 1 — What actually reduces disruption: the proactive stack

The strongest, least ambiguous evidence in this whole area is not about consequences; it is about **structure delivered before misbehavior happens**. The 2024 WWC guide (the newest federal synthesis, and the first behavioral practice guide in 16 years) grades seven teacher-delivered practices:

| Practice | WWC evidence |
|---|---|
| Co-establish, model and **teach** clear expectations (aligned to schoolwide) | **Strong** |
| **Remind** students to engage in expected behaviors (precorrection) | **Strong** |
| Acknowledge expected behavior — positive attention, praise, rewards | **Strong** |
| Use **behavior ratings to give feedback** to students | **Strong** |
| Offer **instructional choice** | Moderate |
| Provide **frequent and varied opportunities to respond (OTR)** | Moderate |
| Teach students to **self-monitor and reflect** | Moderate |

The 2008 guide adds: *modify the classroom learning environment* (**Strong**) and *teach and reinforce new skills* (**Strong**), with *identify the specifics of the problem behavior and its conditions* (Moderate), *draw on colleagues and families* (Moderate), and *assess whether schoolwide strategies are warranted* (Moderate).

Three things worth saying plainly to an administrator:

1. **Precorrection is graded Strong.** A verbal reminder issued *before* the transition or the task is one of the best-evidenced things a teacher does, and it costs nothing. It is under-trained relative to its evidence.
2. **OTR is only Moderate — not because it fails, but because the study base is thin and single-case-heavy.** Simonsen et al. (2008) identified 20 classroom management practices with sufficient evidence — physical arrangement, structure/routines, instructional management, increasing appropriate behavior, decreasing inappropriate behavior — and OTR sits in the instructional-management cluster. Treat OTR as a strong bet with a moderate warrant.
3. **Active supervision** (moving, scanning, interacting) is the practice most commonly taught in this stack that I could **not** locate a WWC-graded warrant for. It rides on descriptive and single-case work, not on a Strong federal grade. Say so; don't inflate it.

Mechanism: all of these operate by **reducing the rate at which unstructured moments arise**, not by changing student dispositions. Transitions, unclear task starts, and wait-time are where disruption is manufactured. Density of teacher-initiated academic responding is inversely related to off-task rate — the classroom management literature's version of "idle hands."

Effect-size context for the broader intervention literature: the classic Stage & Quiron-Ellis-lineage meta-analysis of disruptive-behavior interventions reports a mean around **d ≈ −0.78** on disruptive behavior; more recent RCT-restricted meta-analyses of behavioral classroom programs are far more modest — **d ≈ −0.20** on teacher-rated disruptive behavior and **d ≈ +0.39** on observed on-task behavior. **The measurement method moves the answer:** teacher rating scales show smaller reductions than direct behavioral observation. An administrator reading a vendor's "d = 0.8" claim should ask which of those two worlds it came from.

### Chapter 2 — Teacher–student relationships, and how engagement is actually measured

Roorda, Koomen, Spilt & Oort (2011, *Review of Educational Research*, 99 studies) is the anchor: associations of both **positive and negative** teacher–student relationships with **engagement** are **medium to large**; associations with **achievement** are **small to medium**. The 2017 update tests engagement as the **mediator** — relationship quality appears to work *through* engagement, not around it.

Two honest caveats that must ride along:
- This is a **correlational** literature. Warm relationships and engaged students co-occur; the arrow is not experimentally pinned.
- **Negative relationships carry effects as large as positive ones.** The actionable asymmetry is that removing conflict may buy more than adding warmth.

Corroboration from a completely different design: the Chicago freshman on-track work found students attend class more often **when they have strong relationships with teachers** and see coursework as relevant — and course attendance was **eight times more predictive** of freshman course failure than test scores.

**Measurement.** REL Southeast (REL 2011-098) catalogues **21 distinct engagement instruments** for upper elementary through high school, spanning different theoretical frames (behavioral / emotional / cognitive engagement are not interchangeable). Practical consequence: two schools reporting "engagement" data are frequently measuring different constructs. Before adopting a survey, an administrator should know which of the three engagement facets it loads on — and whether the intended intervention plausibly moves that facet.

### Chapter 3 — The discipline gap: what the data show and what causes it

**Magnitude.** Black students are roughly **16% of enrollment but ~40% of national suspensions** (CRDC-derived, via Brookings). Skiba's *Color of Discipline* line of work establishes the mechanism: disproportionality in **suspension** is largely inherited from prior disproportionality in **office referrals**, and Black students are referred for **less serious and more subjective** reasons. Skiba found **no support** for the hypothesis that Black students act out more; by contrast, boys' higher referral rates were partly attributable to higher rates of the behavior.

**Causal evidence that it is not all composition.** Louisiana: comparing Black and white students in *identical* fights with *no* prior suspensions, Black students received slightly longer suspensions (~**0.04–0.05 additional days**) — small, but a clean identification. North Carolina: Black students received fewer exclusionary consequences when taught by **Black teachers**, concentrated in **subjectively defined** infractions such as "willful defiance." The subjective/objective split is the diagnostic seam — disparities concentrate in the discretionary categories.

**Discretion is where the volume is.** *Breaking Schools' Rules* (≈1M Texas secondary students, ≥6-year follow): **only 3%** of disciplinary actions were for conduct where state law mandated removal. Nearly **6 in 10** students were suspended or expelled at least once between grades 7 and 12. Administratively, this is the single most useful number in the chapter: **97% of the removals are a local policy choice**, not a statutory obligation.

Grade this: **strong descriptive evidence** for the gap; **moderate-to-strong quasi-experimental evidence** that bias/discretion contributes; **not** evidence that behavior differences contribute nothing everywhere.

### Chapter 4 — Exclusionary discipline outcomes: the harms are real, and so are the spillovers

**Correlational/longitudinal.** *Breaking Schools' Rules*: students disciplined 11+ times — only **~40% graduated**, ~**31%** repeated a grade at least once, and juvenile-justice contact rose sharply.

**Causal.** Bacher-Hicks, Billings & Deming (NBER w26257) is the strongest identification available: using a **sudden attendance-boundary change** plus **principal switches** in Charlotte-Mecklenburg, students assigned to a school with a **1 SD higher suspension rate** are **15–20% more likely to be arrested and incarcerated as adults**, with the largest harms to male and minority students. This is causal evidence about **school-level strictness**, not about any individual suspension — an important precision an administrator should not lose.

**And the counterweight that the field routinely underplays.** Philadelphia banned suspensions for non-violent "conduct" offenses in 2012-13. Lacoe & Steinberg found that by 2016, relative to comparison districts, achievement fell roughly **3% in math and ~7% in reading**; truancy, which had been declining, rose from ~25% to over 40%. **Only about a fifth of schools fully complied.** Among *previously suspended* students, achievement rose slightly (~0.2%) and attendance rose **1.43 days/year**. Brookings' read of the same reform: where schools **fully** eliminated conduct suspensions there were **no adverse peer effects**; where compliance was **partial**, peers' attendance and achievement fell slightly.

**The plain statement:** banning a practice is not the same as replacing it. Partial-compliance discipline reform appears to be worse than either endpoint — the removals stop but the replacement practice never arrives, and the cost lands on classmates. This is the chapter's load-bearing finding for administrators.

### Chapter 5 — Restorative practices: null main effects, real subgroup effects, and an academic warning

Two randomized trials, which is more than most of this field has:

**RAND / Pittsburgh (RR-2840, SaferSanerSchools, 2015–17), 44 schools randomized:**
- School climate as rated by **teachers** — improved
- Suspension rates — **reduced**, and reduced **more** for Black students, low-income students, elementary students, and girls
- **Academic outcomes — did not improve, and worsened for grades 6–8**
- **Arrest rates — did not decrease**
- **No** reduction in suspensions for **middle school** students or for **violent** offenses

**Huang, Gregory & Ward-Seidel (*Prevention Science*, cluster RCT, 18 schools, 5,878 students, urban Northeast; 54% Black, 38% Hispanic, 91% low-income, 24% with disabilities):**
- **Main effect on likelihood of suspension: null.** B = −0.001, **p = 0.95**
- **But:** among students **with prior suspensions**, probability of suspension was **11%** in intervention schools vs **33%** in control (**d = 0.77**)
- No differential effects by race, gender, or disability status on overall rates
- Limitations the authors state: one year instead of the planned two (COVID); **four control schools also adopted restorative approaches** (contamination); unclear whether *student behavior* or *staff response* changed

**Grade: mixed, with a specific shape.** Restorative practices have the best evidence for **reducing exclusion among students already in the exclusion pipeline**, weaker evidence for whole-population suspension reduction, **no** demonstrated academic benefit, and **one trial showing academic harm in middle grades**. Anyone selling restorative practice as an achievement intervention is going beyond the evidence. The middle-school result recurs across both trials and should not be smoothed over.

### Chapter 6 — Academic RTI/MTSS: screening, tiers, decision rules — and the federal evaluation that found harm

**The framework's own practice guides do not grade well.** WWC PG #3 (reading RTI, 2009):

| Recommendation | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Screen all students for reading problems | Moderate |
| Provide **differentiated** reading instruction (Tier 1) | **Minimal** |
| Provide intensive systematic instruction in small groups (Tier 2) | **Strong** |
| Monitor Tier 2 progress monthly | **Minimal** |
| Provide intensive daily Tier 3 instruction | **Minimal** |

The math RTI guide (PG #2) splits similarly: 2 Strong, 3 Moderate, **3 Minimal**. And the data-use guide (PG #12, 2009) is the starkest — **all five recommendations rated Minimal Evidence**, including the entire data-team/data-culture apparatus that districts have spent fifteen years building.

**Then the national evaluation.** IES/NCEE 2016-4000 (Balu, Zhu, Doolittle, Schiller, Jenkins, Gersten), 146 schools **experienced with RTI** across 13 states, 2011-12, **regression-discontinuity around each school's own screening cut point**:
- **86%** of experienced schools reported full implementation
- **Grade 1: students just below the cut point who were assigned to intervention had LOWER spring reading scores than students just above it** — a negative impact on the order of **~one-tenth of a year of learning**
- **Grades 2 and 3: no statistically significant impacts**, positive or negative
- **Impacts varied significantly across schools**
- 55% of schools concentrated services on struggling Grade 1 readers; 45% also served students at or above grade level

**Say this plainly.** The largest federal evaluation of RTI as actually practiced found **no benefit in grades 2–3 and harm in grade 1** at the margin. The estimate is **local to the cut point** — it says nothing about students far below benchmark, who were not identified by this design. But the marginal student is precisely the student the screening cut point exists to decide about, so this is the most policy-relevant estimand available, not a technicality.

**Candidate mechanisms** (the report and the field's response point at these, none established): marginal Grade 1 students were **pulled out of high-quality Tier 1 core instruction** to receive Tier 2 that was no better; grouping marginal students with much weaker peers reduced instructional match; and intervention **displaced** rather than supplemented core reading time. Note that the one recommendation graded **Strong** in the reading guide — intensive systematic small-group instruction — is precisely the one whose *content quality* is not guaranteed by the framework. **RTI is a decision architecture, not an intervention.** A well-run architecture routing students into weak instruction produces exactly this result.

### Chapter 7 — Screening tools, progress monitoring, and data-based individualization

This is where the field's best free technical infrastructure lives, and it is almost entirely NCII (OSEP-funded, all free).

**Universal screening — what a cut point actually is.** NCII's Academic Screening Tools Chart rates 100+ tools on **classification accuracy**: **AUC ≥ 0.80** for a full rating, and **sensitivity ≥ 0.80 and specificity ≥ 0.80**. Plus reliability, validity, **sample representativeness** (national/regional/local), **bias analysis across demographic subgroups**, and usability. The chart explicitly is **not an endorsement** — tools appear regardless of rating.

The substance an administrator needs: a screening cut point is a **sensitivity/specificity trade**, not a fact about a child. Moving the cut to catch more true risk necessarily generates more false positives — and given the Grade 1 finding above, **false positives are not free**. A school setting its benchmark at the 40th percentile "to be safe" is knowingly assigning a large group of near-benchmark students to an intervention with no demonstrated marginal benefit and evidence of marginal harm. Local norms vs national norms materially change who is flagged.

**Progress monitoring.** NCII's academic progress-monitoring chart rates **reliability of slope** (lower bound of the CI around the median coefficient **≥ 0.50**, based on **≥10 measurements over ≥20 weeks**) and **validity of slope** (≥ 0.40 against an external criterion), plus **growth standards** (weekly rates of improvement by grade + end-of-year benchmarks) and **decision rules**. The reliability-of-slope bar is the fact that should change practice: **you need on the order of 10 data points across 20 weeks before an individual student's slope is a trustworthy quantity.** Districts making instructional change decisions off 4–6 weekly probes are reading noise. This is a checkable, quantitative rebuttal to a near-universal practice.

**Data-based individualization (DBI)** — the 5-step cycle: (1) validated intervention program → (2) progress monitoring → (3) if unresponsive, **diagnostic assessment** (or FBA on the behavior side) → (4) **intervention adaptation** → (5) continued progress monitoring, looping back to diagnostic assessment on non-response. Intensity is specified via the **Taxonomy of Intervention Intensity** — **seven dimensions** (strength, dosage, alignment, attention to transfer, comprehensiveness, behavioral support, individualization). The taxonomy's contribution is that "more intensive" stops meaning "more minutes": it decomposes intensity into dimensions you can move independently.

**A real gap worth flagging:** the **behavior** screening tools chart currently rates **one** tool (SAEBRS/FAST, K–5, teacher-rated). Universal behavioral screening has nothing like the technical infrastructure that academic screening has. Districts adopting behavior screeners are largely operating without a vetted comparison.

### Chapter 8 — Differentiation, learning styles, and grouping: popularity vs evidence

**Learning styles: debunked. State it flatly.** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 9(3):105–119) tested the **meshing hypothesis** — that instruction is most effective when its format matches the learner's stated style preference. The required design is a **crossed interaction**: classify learners by style, randomly assign to instructional formats, and show that the format×style interaction is significant with each group doing best in its matched format. Pashler et al. found **one** study giving even partial support and **two** that flatly contradicted it. Their conclusion: **no adequate evidence base justifies incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice.** A later APA-published study (Rogowsky et al.) matching learning style to instructional method likewise found **no** comprehension benefit. Their own honest caveat, which should be carried: most versions of learning styles have simply **never been properly tested**, so "all versions are false" overstates it — but the popular, purchasable, teacher-training version has been tested and failed. Belief in it remains near-ubiquitous among educators (Nancekivell et al., 2020, documents this persistence).

**Differentiated instruction: popular, under-evidenced, and not one thing.** Smale-Jacobse et al. (2019, *Frontiers in Psychology*, open access) searched 2006–2016 secondary education and found **only 14 papers on 12 unique empirical studies**, yielding 15 effects. Reported effects on achievement are mostly small to moderate positive (**summarized d ≈ +0.51 to +0.74** after omitting an outlier — and one mathematics study reported an implausible d > +4.5, which is itself a signal about study quality). Their own conclusion: **"more research is needed before drawing convincing conclusions."**

The deeper problem is **construct incoherence**. "Differentiated instruction" in that review covers: generic teacher training, ability grouping and tiering, individualization, mastery learning, heterogeneous grouping, and remediation in flipped classrooms. These have different mechanisms and different effects. **An effect size for "differentiation" is close to meaningless.** Consistent with this, WWC graded "provide differentiated reading instruction" as **Minimal Evidence** in the RTI reading guide. Grade: **weak/indeterminate evidence for the branded practice; specific components (mastery learning, small-group intensive instruction) have their own, better warrants.** Differentiation-by-learning-style is the component that is affirmatively refuted.

**Grouping and tracking — the effects depend entirely on which kind.** Steenbergen-Hu, Makel & Olszewski-Kubilius (2016, *RER* 86(4):849–899), second-order meta-analysis over several dozen syntheses covering ~300 original studies:

| Arrangement | Effect (g) |
|---|---|
| **Within-class** grouping | +0.19 to +0.30 |
| **Cross-grade subject** grouping (e.g., Joplin plan) | +0.26 |
| **Special grouping for the gifted** | +0.37 |
| **Between-class** grouping / tracking | **+0.04 to +0.06 (≈ null)** |
| Acceleration vs same-age peers | +0.70 |
| Acceleration vs older non-accelerated peers | +0.09 |
| Acceleration, aggregated | +0.42 |

And the finding that kills the most common objection: **effects did not vary for high-, medium-, and low-ability students.** So the empirical picture is: **flexible, skill-specific, within-class or cross-grade grouping works modestly; whole-school between-class tracking does essentially nothing academically.** The case against tracking is therefore an **equity and opportunity-structure** case, not an "it lowers achievement" case — which is a different and more defensible argument. The acceleration result (+0.70 vs same-age peers, ~+0.09 vs older peers) is best read as *accelerated students perform like the older students they joined* — that is success, not fade.

### Chapter 9 — Structural levers: class size, instructional time, early warning

**Class size (Tennessee Project STAR).** The design: ~6,500–11,571 students across **79 schools**, students **and teachers** randomly assigned within school to small (13–17), regular, or regular-with-aide classes, K–3, 1985–1989. This is still the best randomized evidence in American education policy.
- Contemporaneous effects large enough to matter for policy, consistent across schools, benefiting all student types (Finn & Achilles; Nye/Hedges/Konstantopoulos)
- Nye et al. (1999): effects persist **at least five years** after students return to regular classes
- Krueger & Whitmore (NBER w7656): small-class students more likely to **take a college-entrance exam**, especially minority students
- Chetty, Friedman, Hilger, Saez, Schanzenbach & Yagan (2011, *QJE* 126(4)): linking STAR to tax records — small-class students significantly more likely to **attend college**; kindergarten class *quality* (teacher experience, classroom peer effects) predicts **adult earnings at 27**, home ownership, retirement savings, even where **test-score gains faded**

**The limits an administrator must hear.** (a) STAR reduced class size by ~7–8 students in **K–3 only**; nothing licenses extrapolation to a 30→27 reduction in high school. (b) Scaling is where it dies — California's 1996 statewide CSR forced mass hiring of uncertified teachers, and the teacher-quality loss plausibly swamped the class-size gain; Hoxby's Connecticut population-variation study found **no** effect. Agostinelli and colleagues' STAR reappraisal (heterogeneity and endogenous compliance) makes the scaling problem explicit. (c) Test-score fade-out is real; the adult-outcome persistence is the surprising part, and it implies the mechanism is **not** the tested content. Grade: **strong internal validity, weak external validity, terrible cost-effectiveness at scale.**

**Instructional time.** Kraft & Novicoff (2024, *AERJ*): causal effects of additional time on achievement are **positive but small-to-medium**, dependent on dosage, use and context; roughly, **a ≥10% increase in total time** is what it takes to produce small achievement increases in the U.S. context. The more useful finding is the **variation and the leakage**: instructional time across U.S. public schools has a **90th–10th percentile gap of 190 total hours**, and schools face systematic obstacles converting *total* time into *active learning* time — the student's own effort and motivation, the teacher's and school's ability to motivate, the curriculum, and **peers' behavior**. That last term is the hinge that ties this chapter back to Chapter 1: **classroom management is an instructional-time intervention.** Recovering lost minutes inside the existing day is cheaper than lengthening it and does not require a calendar fight.

**Early warning as the engagement instrument that actually works.** Allensworth & Easton (Chicago, 2005/2007): a **dichotomous** on-track indicator — enough credits to be promoted to 10th grade and **no more than one semester F in a core course** — predicts graduation with **~80% accuracy**. On-track students were **four times** more likely to graduate. Course performance indicators were **far more predictive than test scores or background characteristics**, and **attendance was eight times more predictive of course failure than test scores**. Note the tension with WWC PG #24, which grades "monitor progress and proactively intervene on early attendance/behavior/academic signs" at only **Minimal Evidence** — the *prediction* is excellent; the *intervention that follows the flag* is what lacks a warrant. Same structural lesson as RTI: **flagging is not treating.**

## 4. Use-cases for a K-12 administrator

Distinct, not blurred:

1. **Choosing what to put in required classroom-management PD.** Rank by WWC grade, not by popularity: expectations-taught, precorrection, acknowledgment, and behavior-rating feedback are **Strong**; choice, OTR and self-monitoring are **Moderate**; learning-styles training is **refuted and should be removed from the catalogue**.
2. **Auditing a discipline code before a board meeting.** Determine what fraction of your removals are **statutorily mandated** vs discretionary. Texas's answer was **3%**. Then split referrals into **objective vs subjective** categories and check disproportionality separately in each — that is where bias concentrates.
3. **Deciding whether to reform discipline policy — and how far.** The Philadelphia evidence says **partial compliance is the worst outcome**. Either fully resource the replacement practice or do not start. Plan compliance monitoring as part of the policy, not as an afterthought.
4. **Evaluating a restorative-practices vendor proposal.** Ask for effects on: suspension rates overall (expect small/null), suspensions among **previously suspended** students (expect real), **middle school** suspensions (expect null), **academic outcomes** (expect null, and be alert for harm in grades 6–8), and arrests (expect null). Any proposal promising achievement gains is exceeding the evidence.
5. **Setting or revising universal screening cut points.** Treat the cut as a **sensitivity/specificity trade** with a real cost on the false-positive side. Verify the tool's AUC and sub-group **bias analysis** on the NCII chart before adoption, and check whether the norms are national or local.
6. **Designing progress-monitoring decision rules.** Require **~10 data points over ~20 weeks** before acting on an individual slope. Kill four-week "data-driven" instructional changes; they are noise-driven. Use NCII's slope-reliability criterion as the district standard.
7. **Diagnosing an underperforming Tier 2 program.** Run the **Taxonomy of Intervention Intensity** (7 dimensions) rather than adding minutes. Then check the question the IES evaluation raises: **what is the intervention displacing?** If Tier 2 is pulling students out of strong Tier 1 core instruction, you may be manufacturing the Grade 1 result.
8. **Answering "should we buy a differentiation initiative?"** Ask which specific component is being sold. Mastery learning and small-group intensive instruction have warrants; "differentiation" as a brand does not; style-matching is refuted.
9. **Setting a grouping policy.** Adopt flexible **within-class** and **cross-grade subject** grouping (g ≈ +0.19 to +0.30). Do not expect achievement gains from whole-school **between-class tracking** (g ≈ +0.05). Make the tracking argument on equity grounds, which the evidence supports; do not claim achievement harm it does not show.
10. **Responding to a class-size reduction proposal.** Cite STAR honestly: real, randomized, K–3, large reduction, persistent adult outcomes — **and** the scaling failure (teacher-quality dilution) that makes district-wide CSR a poor buy. Ask where the teachers come from before asking what the effect size is.
11. **Recovering instructional time without lengthening the day.** Kraft's 190-hour 90th/10th spread and lost-time analysis makes internal time audits (transitions, start-of-class latency, interruptions, assemblies) a defensible improvement lever with a lower political cost than calendar change.
12. **Standing up an early-warning system.** Use the Chicago on-track construction (credits + ≤1 core semester F; attendance as the leading indicator). Budget for the **response**, not just the dashboard — WWC grades the monitoring recommendation **Minimal**, and the prediction is worthless without a treatment attached.
13. **Selecting an engagement survey.** Use REL 2011-098's catalogue of 21 instruments and pick on **construct** (behavioral / emotional / cognitive) matched to the intervention you intend. Do not benchmark against another district using a different instrument.
14. **Writing an ESSA evidence-tier justification for a funding application.** WWC practice-guide evidence levels and the WWC ESSA tiers page give defensible, free, citable grades.

## 5. Cross-links to existing primers

- **`applied-behavior-analysis-and-behavioral-intervention`** — PBIS/SWPBS, FBA/BIP, reinforcement functions, token economies, single-case designs. **Not re-covered here.** This primer carries only what a classroom teacher/administrator needs on top: the WWC-graded *teacher-delivered* practice set (precorrection, OTR, behavior-rating feedback), the discipline-gap and exclusionary-outcomes evidence, and the restorative-practice trials. Note the seam: **WWC PG #4 rec. 1** (identify the specifics of the problem behavior and its conditions) is classroom-level FBA and belongs to the ABA primer; **WWC PG #4 rec. 5** (assess whether schoolwide strategies are warranted) is the handoff point to SWPBS. NCII's **behavior** screening chart and behavioral progress-monitoring charts also live closer to ABA.
- **`school-counseling-practice`** — MTSS tiers from the counselor's side. This primer covers the **academic/instructional** tiers: screening cut points, tiered *instruction*, progress-monitoring decision rules, DBI, and the IES RTI evaluation. Shared object, different face: the **early-warning system** (attendance/behavior/course-performance flags) is jointly owned — the indicator design sits here, the Tier 2/3 response often sits with counseling.
- **`learning-science-foundations`** — cognitive load, scaffolding and fading, motivation, self-regulation theory. This primer's **self-monitoring** (WWC Moderate), **instructional choice** (Moderate), and **engagement** constructs are the classroom-implementation face of that theory. The **learning-styles** entry belongs in both: theory-side as a refuted individual-differences construct, practice-side as the specific differentiation component that is affirmatively debunked.
- **`education-law`** — *Goss v. Lopez* due process, IDEA discipline provisions (manifestation determination, the 10-day rule), OCR Dear Colleague guidance on disparate impact in discipline. The **Texas 3%-mandatory finding** is the empirical bridge: it quantifies how much of school discipline is legal obligation vs local discretion. Disproportionality data here is the factual predicate for disparate-impact analysis there.
- **`neurodiversity-in-daily-life`** — the ADHD classroom-intervention meta-analytic results (consequence-based and self-regulation interventions largest) and the 24%-students-with-disabilities composition of the restorative-practices RCT sample connect here; also the disproportionality-by-disability finding in *Breaking Schools' Rules*.
- **`developmental-psychology`** — the recurring **middle-grades anomaly** (restorative practices ineffective or harmful in grades 6–8; the Chicago freshman transition as a discontinuity) is best explained from the developmental side, not the instructional side.
