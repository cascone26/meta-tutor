# Stage R — Early Literacy Instruction: Science of Reading, Structured Literacy, Screening, Reading MTSS

This is the K-12 instructional layer a principal actually manages: how children are taught to read in K-3, why the field publicly reversed itself between roughly 2019 and 2025, what the screening-and-intervention machinery is, and where the "settled science" is genuinely settled versus where it is still an argument. It is written to sit next to R3 (curriculum/standards/assessment) and R4 (classroom management / academic MTSS) without repeating them — R4 owns the generic Tier 1/2/3 RTI machinery and the NCII tools charts; this file owns the *reading-specific* content that fills those tiers.

Two honesty rules run through it. First, the evidence for **explicit, systematic foundational-skills instruction** is genuinely strong (multiple federal meta-analyses, WWC "Strong" ratings) and should be stated plainly. Second, the Science of Reading has become a **movement and a brand**, and several of its downstream claims (advanced phonemic awareness, exact phonics dosage, LETRS causal impact, dyslexia-screener specificity) are weaker than the movement's marketing implies. A principal who cannot separate the two will either under-buy the strong part or over-promise the weak part.

## 2. Findings — candidate chapters

### Chapter 1 — The theory stack: Simple View, Reading Rope, and the "Big Five"

Three models sit under everything a principal will hear said in a Science-of-Reading PD, and they nest cleanly.

**The Simple View of Reading (Gough & Tunmer, 1986).** Reading Comprehension = Decoding × Language Comprehension. The single most consequential feature is that the operation is **multiplication, not addition**: if either factor is zero the product is zero, so a fluent decoder who cannot understand spoken language, and a strong listener who cannot decode print, are *both* non-readers, for opposite reasons. This is not a slogan — it is a testable model, and Reading Rockets and the Hoover/Tunmer scholarly update (ERIC EJ1191985) both report it has been examined in well over 100 studies with broad (not universal) empirical support. The operational payoff is diagnostic: it yields **three poor-reader profiles** — a decoding problem, a language-comprehension problem, or both — and those map to different interventions. A district that screens only decoding will miss the language-comprehension poor readers entirely (the "word callers" who decode fluently in grade 2 and collapse in grade 4 when text demands background knowledge). **Evidence grade: strong as a framework; note that critics argue "simple" understates the role of fluency and that the two components are less separable in practice than the formula implies.**

**Scarborough's Reading Rope (2001).** The Rope expands the Simple View's two factors into strands. The **word-recognition** braid: phonological awareness, decoding, sight recognition — strands that become *increasingly automatic* over time. The **language-comprehension** braid: background knowledge, vocabulary, language structures, verbal reasoning, literacy knowledge — strands that become *increasingly strategic*. The two braids tighten into skilled, fluent reading. The Rope's instructional value is that it makes the Simple View's abstractions curricular: each strand is something you can teach, screen, and intervene on. **Evidence grade: the Rope is a heuristic, not an empirically-tested causal model — it is a widely adopted *organizer* of the underlying research, and should be presented as such, not as itself a study.**

**The "Big Five" (National Reading Panel, 2000).** The NRP's *Teaching Children to Read* named five components with a research base: **phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, comprehension.** This is the workhorse framework — the pillars every state SoR statute and every screener is organized around. The NRP's own meta-analytic effect sizes (Ehri et al. 2001, the technical papers behind the report) are the numbers a principal should actually carry:

- **Phonemic awareness instruction:** large effect on PA itself (**d = 0.86**), moderate on reading (**d = 0.53**), moderate on spelling (**d = 0.59**). PA instruction worked best when (a) taught with **letters**, not oral-only, (b) focused on **one or two** manipulations rather than many, and (c) delivered in **small groups**.
- **Systematic phonics:** overall reading effect **d = 0.41** (38 experiments, 66 comparisons), larger for younger children and for struggling/at-risk readers, with the important caveat that comprehension gains attenuate in older grades. No single phonics program beat the others — *systematic* beat *unsystematic/none*, but synthetic vs. analytic phonics did not clearly separate.
- **Fluency:** guided repeated oral reading has a documented positive effect on word recognition, fluency, and comprehension; independent silent reading (DEAR/SSR) was **not** supported by the panel as a standalone method for building fluency in developing readers.
- **Vocabulary and comprehension:** the panel endorsed direct + indirect vocabulary instruction and *multiple-strategy* comprehension instruction, but the evidence base here is thinner and messier than for the code-based components — a fact the movement tends to gloss.

**The honest through-line for the primer:** the *code* side of the Big Five (PA + phonics) has the strongest, most replicated evidence; the *meaning* side (vocabulary, comprehension) is real but weaker and more dependent on content knowledge. The NICHD and Reading Rockets summary pages, both verified live, state the findings **qualitatively with no effect sizes** — the numbers above come from the Ehri technical papers, and a PD deck that quotes "the science" should be able to name them.

### Chapter 2 — Structured literacy and Orton-Gillingham: the practice model, and where its evidence is softer than advertised

**Structured Literacy** is the International Dyslexia Association's umbrella term for instruction that is **explicit, systematic, cumulative, diagnostic, and multisensory**, covering phonology, sound-symbol association, syllables, morphology, syntax, and semantics. It is best understood as the *instructional operationalization* of the Big Five for the students who most need it — and the IDA names it as the recommended approach for dyslexia. As a **description of good code-based teaching**, it inherits the strong NRP evidence base. That much is defensible.

**Orton-Gillingham (OG)** is the century-old branded approach structured literacy descends from — multisensory, sequential, prescriptive. Here the primer has to be careful, because OG is where the marketing outruns the evidence. The most rigorous synthesis is **Stevens et al. (2021)** in *Exceptional Children* (24 studies): OG interventions produced a **small and statistically non-significant** effect on foundational skills (reported around **g = 0.22, p = .40**) and a small non-significant effect on reading comprehension. Older, less-controlled reviews report larger figures (decoding d ≈ 0.47, spelling d ≈ 0.40, comprehension d ≈ 0.32), and both circulate. The reconciliation: **the principles OG embodies (explicit, systematic, cumulative) are well-supported; OG-as-a-specific-branded-program is not proven superior to other structured, explicit approaches, and the meta-analytic estimate for OG per se is not statistically distinguishable from zero.** Separately, **Schlesinger & Gray (2017)** directly compared structured literacy with and without the **multisensory** component and found no added benefit from the multisensory element — meaning the "multisensory" selling point specifically lacks support even though the structured/explicit core is sound.

**Practical translation for a principal:** buy the *properties* (explicit, systematic, cumulative, diagnostic), not the *brand*. Do not pay a premium for "OG-certified" or "multisensory" on the assumption the label carries independent efficacy. **Evidence grade: strong for structured/explicit instruction as a class; weak/contested for OG-as-brand and for the multisensory feature specifically.** Route the special-education *entitlement* questions (is OG "required" for a dyslexic student's IEP?) to `education-law`; this primer answers only the instructional-quality question.

### Chapter 3 — Why balanced literacy fell: three-cueing, the reading wars, and "Sold a Story"

This is the chapter that explains the political weather a 2026 administrator is standing in, and it must be told as history, not as a morality tale.

**What balanced literacy was.** For roughly three decades the dominant US approach descended from Ken Goodman's whole-language and Marie Clay's Reading Recovery. Its engine was **three-cueing (MSV)** — teaching children to identify an unknown word by cross-checking three "cues": **M**eaning (context/pictures), **S**yntax (does it sound right), and **V**isual (letters), typically in that priority order. In practice this trained beginning readers to **guess from context and pictures first and consult the letters last**. The market leaders were Lucy Calkins' Units of Study (Teachers College Reading and Writing Project) and Fountas & Pinnell (guided reading, leveled texts, running records). By 2020 an estimated ~75% of US teachers reported using three-cueing.

**Why researchers say it is discredited.** The core empirical finding, going back to **Keith Stanovich in the 1970s**, is that reliance on context to identify words is characteristic of **weak** readers, not strong ones — skilled readers recognize words so fast (via orthographic mapping) that they don't *need* context to decode, and use context only for comprehension. Eye-tracking work reinforced that fluent readers fixate nearly every word and process its letters. Three-cueing, by teaching guessing, is argued to **interfere with the orthographic mapping** that produces automatic word recognition — producing children who confuse *horse/house* or *pony/horse* because they were cued to the picture, not the print. **Evidence grade on the specific claim "three-cueing is harmful": the correlational and cognitive evidence that skilled reading is not context-guessing is strong; a clean RCT showing three-cueing *causes* worse outcomes than a phonics-first control is harder to point to, so state it as "contradicts the established model of skilled word recognition," not as a proven causal harm.**

**The catalyst.** Emily Hanford's reporting, culminating in the American Public Media podcast **"Sold a Story" (2022)**, moved the argument from research journals into legislatures and living rooms, tying the national reading crisis to three-cueing curricula. The commercial and political fallout was fast: Calkins revised Units of Study; Teachers College closed the Reading and Writing Project; and states began legislating (Chapter 5). A principal should understand this as a *reason for community pressure* — parents now arrive citing a podcast — and hold the neutral line: the direction of travel (toward explicit code instruction) is well-supported, but the reversal has movement-and-brand dynamics, and "balanced literacy" contained some genuinely good practices (read-alouds, wide reading, writing) that are being thrown out alongside three-cueing. The defensible position is **"keep the explicit code instruction and the knowledge-building; drop the guessing,"** not "everything with the balanced-literacy label was worthless."

### Chapter 4 — Decodable vs. predictable/leveled texts

The text-type question is the concrete classroom expression of Chapter 3, and the evidence is real but narrower than either camp claims.

**The two text types.** **Decodable** texts restrict vocabulary to the grapheme-phoneme correspondences a child has already been taught, so a beginner can *decode* rather than guess (e.g., after teaching short-a and /s/,/t/,/m/, the text uses only words buildable from those). **Predictable/leveled** texts (Fountas & Pinnell levels, "patterned" books) repeat a sentence frame with picture support ("I see a cat / I see a dog"), which invites exactly the picture-and-context guessing three-cueing trained.

**What the evidence supports.** Children reading decodable text **attempt to sound words out more** and rely less on picture/context guessing than children reading predictable text — a well-replicated behavioral finding, and the mechanism the Science of Reading predicts. There is meta-analytic support that decodables improve phonics knowledge and pseudoword decoding (the cleanest index of decoding skill). **But** — and Timothy Shanahan, an NRP panelist and *internal critic*, is the honest voice here — the rigorous evidence that decodables produce **superior long-run reading achievement** over other text choices is **thin**, and there is likewise "scant evidence" validating the leveling systems (F&P levels) as accurate or instructionally effective. The defensible synthesis: **decodables are the right tool during the initial code-learning window because they force the target strategy; they are a temporary scaffold, not a permanent diet, and the field lacks strong evidence about the exact transition point to authentic text.** **Evidence grade: moderate for decodables during early phonics instruction; weak for strong long-term achievement claims; weak/negative for leveled-text leveling systems.**

### Chapter 5 — State early-literacy legislation: what is actually being mandated

This is the fastest-moving policy area in K-12 and the one most likely to change what a principal must do between one school year and the next. The reliable frame is that by 2024–25 the great majority of states had passed some Science-of-Reading statute; the components vary, and a principal must read *their own state's* law, not the national summary.

**The four common statutory levers** (ExcelinEd in Action's 2024 review, fetched live):
1. **Three-cueing bans.** At least **7 states banned or restricted three-cueing in 2024 alone** (Alabama, Indiana, Kansas, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Virginia, plus Arizona/Kansas curriculum provisions); Wikipedia's tracker puts the cumulative total at **more than 12 states**, with ~45 states having passed *some* reading-reform bill.
2. **Universal K-3 screening mandates** (≥6 states added or tightened in 2024: Georgia, Indiana, Minnesota, New Jersey, Oklahoma, South Carolina — South Carolina now requires **3× yearly** screening).
3. **Curriculum / "evidence-based" instruction requirements** (9+ states emphasized SoR-aligned curriculum or approved-list adoption; note the overlap with R3's HQIM machinery — early-literacy laws often *are* HQIM adoption laws with a reading label).
4. **Teacher training mandates** (LETRS or equivalent microcredentials; e.g., Kansas SoR microcredentials, Oklahoma competency exam).

**Third-grade retention ("read-by-3") laws** deserve their own honest treatment because they are the sharpest and most contested lever. States including **Florida, Arizona, Arkansas, Indiana, South Carolina, Mississippi, Tennessee** condition promotion to 4th grade on a reading benchmark, with **good-cause exemptions** (ELs, IEPs, prior retention, portfolio evidence). The evidence, from the Florida natural experiment (**Schwerdt, West & Winters**, RD design on ~1M students): retention produced **large short-run achievement gains that faded to statistical insignificance by ~6 years**, but also **higher high-school GPA, fewer remedial courses, and no negative effect on graduation** — i.e., not the developmental catastrophe retention's critics feared, and not the durable fix its advocates claim. NEPC and others contest the study's validity (the RD compares near-miss students, and remediation is bundled with retention, so you cannot cleanly attribute effects to being held back vs. the intervention that accompanies it). **Evidence grade: retention effects are genuinely mixed and the causal attribution is contested; the defensible statement is that test-based retention *plus intensive remediation* does not appear to harm long-run outcomes and may modestly help, but retention-alone is not supported and the policy is as much political as evidentiary.** Route the legal/equity exposure to `education-law`.

### Chapter 6 — Universal screening and dyslexia screening

Screening is the operational heart of reading MTSS, and by 2020 roughly **47 states required early dyslexia screening** — so this is a compliance obligation, not an option.

**What a reading screener is.** A brief (1–5 min), repeatable measure given to **all** students (typically 3× yearly: fall/winter/spring) to flag who is below a benchmark and needs a closer look. The dominant instruments are curriculum-based measures (CBMs):
- **DIBELS 8th Edition** (University of Oregon; the **measures are free**, the Amplify data-management system is paid). Subtests: Letter Naming Fluency (LNF), Phoneme Segmentation Fluency (PSF), Nonsense Word Fluency (NWF), Word Reading Fluency (WRF), Oral Reading Fluency (ORF), Maze. DIBELS maps its subtests to dyslexia risk indicators: **LNF → rapid automatized naming, PSF → phonemic awareness, NWF → decoding**, per the University of Oregon dyslexia white paper.
- **Acadience Reading** — the rebranded/continued **DIBELS Next** lineage (same authors, diverged from the free DIBELS 8 line); paid.
- **aimswebPlus** (Pearson) — a paid, normed CBM + computer-adaptive battery.
- Others in the ecosystem: **easyCBM** (free), **FastBridge**, **i-Ready**, **STAR**.

**The honest evaluation frame — do not let a vendor pick your screener.** The neutral authority is the **NCII Academic Screening Tools Chart** (free, verified in R4), which rates ~100+ tools on **classification accuracy** (AUC / sensitivity / specificity, with ≥0.80 as the informal bar), reliability, validity, and disaggregated bias analysis. The principal-relevant subtlety is the **sensitivity/specificity trade-off**: a screener tuned for high sensitivity (catches nearly every true struggling reader) will over-identify (many false positives → wasted Tier 2 slots), while one tuned for specificity under-identifies (misses real cases). There is **no screener that is simultaneously highly sensitive and highly specific**, so the choice is a values decision about which error you can tolerate, made at the district level. **Evidence grade: CBM screening as a class is well-validated for *predicting* reading risk; but a screener is a risk flag, not a diagnosis — dyslexia "screening" laws frequently blur this, and a positive screen must route to diagnostic assessment, not to a label.** IES is still *validating* DIBELS 8 specifically as a dyslexia screener (active award), which is the tell that even the market-leading tool's dyslexia-specific validity is a work in progress.

### Chapter 7 — MTSS for reading: filling the tiers with the right content

R4 owns the generic RTI/MTSS machinery and the NCII tools charts; this chapter supplies only the **reading-specific** content that goes inside the tiers, and the one federal document that governs it.

**The governing guide is WWC Practice Guide #21, *Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in K–3*** (2016, rev. 2019; free, 6.2 MB, no login — fetched live). Its four recommendations and their evidence levels are the single most useful thing in this whole sub-area because they tell a principal exactly where the evidence is strong:
1. Teach **academic language skills** (inferential/narrative language, vocabulary) — **Minimal** evidence.
2. Develop **phonological awareness** — **Strong** evidence.
3. Teach **decoding and word analysis** — **Strong** evidence.
4. Ensure students read **connected text daily** to build accuracy, fluency, comprehension — **Moderate** evidence.

So the two code-based recommendations carry WWC's top rating; the language/vocabulary recommendation carries its lowest — the same asymmetry as Chapter 1. This is the honest core: **the part of early reading with the strongest evidence is exactly the part balanced literacy neglected.**

**The tier structure, reading-specific** (WWC PG#3 + the Massachusetts DESE tiered-instruction pages, both leads):
- **Tier 1** — core reading instruction for all, in an HQIM aligned to the Big Five, in the general classroom.
- **Tier 2** — supplemental **small-group** (typically 3–5×/week, 20–40 min) instruction targeting **up to three** specific foundational skills, for students below the screening benchmark. WWC's own caution: the primary-grades RTI-reading recs historically carried *minimal-to-moderate* evidence, and IES's national RD evaluation of RTI (NCEE 2016-4000, cited in R4) even found a **negative Grade 1 impact** where Tier 2 pulled students *out* of Tier 1 core instruction — a real implementation warning, not an argument against tiering.
- **Tier 3** — **intensive, daily, individualized** intervention for non-responders, ideally via **data-based individualization** (NCII's DBI), with more frequent progress monitoring.

**For grades 4–9** (older students who missed the K-3 window), the guide is **WWC PG#29 (2022)** — different content emphasis (morphology, multisyllabic decoding, comprehension/vocabulary in content areas), because the intervention target shifts once basic code should be in place. **Evidence grade: the *content* to put in the tiers is well-specified by PG#21 (two Strong recs); the *tiering delivery system* carries weaker evidence and is implementation-sensitive — the failure mode is replacing, rather than adding to, Tier 1.**

### Chapter 8 — LETRS and teacher knowledge: the training layer, honestly graded

The Science-of-Reading movement's theory of change is that the binding constraint is **teacher knowledge** — most teachers were trained in balanced-literacy programs and never learned the linguistics of English orthography. The dominant remedy is **LETRS (Language Essentials for Teachers of Reading and Spelling)**, a ~2-year, expensive professional-learning course (Lexia/Voyager Sopris); roughly **450,000 US educators** took it between 2019 and 2022, often under state mandate.

**The evidence, graded honestly.** IES's own program page states plainly that despite LETRS's massive adoption, **causal evidence of its impact on teacher knowledge, practice, and student outcomes is limited** — which is why IES is *currently funding* the definitive study. The widely-cited **Kentucky study (2026)** analyzed 574 schools / 135 districts / 7,000+ educators and found schools with above-average LETRS-trained-teacher concentration had larger proficiency gains (4.26% vs 2.90% growth in "Proficient," 2023→2025). Read it precisely: this is a **correlational, school-level** finding — schools that invested heavily in LETRS differ from those that didn't in many ways (leadership, resources, motivation), so it cannot isolate LETRS's causal effect, and it is produced within the state that mandated the training. **Evidence grade: the *premise* (teacher content knowledge matters, and many teachers lack it) is well-supported; the *specific efficacy of LETRS as a program* on student outcomes is not yet established by rigorous causal evidence — the strongest current data are correlational and the federal clearinghouse is still studying it.** The administrator translation: teacher knowledge-building is worth investing in, but do not treat the LETRS brand as proven ROI; cheaper and faster knowledge-building routes exist (FCRR courses, Reading League materials — Chapter 9), and the mechanism that matters (teachers understanding the code) is not proprietary to any one program.

### Chapter 9 — What is genuinely settled vs. still contested (the honesty chapter)

The phrase "the settled science of reading" is doing a lot of rhetorical work, and a principal needs the real map so as not to over-claim to a board.

**Settled (strong, replicated evidence):**
- Skilled word recognition is achieved through **orthographic mapping**, not context-guessing; the skilled-reader-does-not-guess finding (Stanovich onward) is robust.
- **Explicit, systematic phonics** beats unsystematic/no phonics for beginning and struggling readers (NRP d ≈ 0.41; WWC "Strong").
- **Phonemic awareness** instruction helps, especially **taught with letters** (NRP d = 0.86 on PA, 0.53 on reading).
- The **Simple View** — reading requires *both* decoding and language comprehension — is a strong organizing model.

**Contested / unsettled (state as open, per the Hechinger controversies piece, fetched live, and Shanahan):**
- **"Advanced" phonemic awareness** (oral-only phoneme deletion/substitution "in the dark," à la Kilpatrick's *phonemic proficiency* hypothesis). **Clemens et al. (2021)** — nine reading researchers — argued that none of the studies cited to justify advanced oral PA drills actually isolate that ingredient, and that the meta-analytic evidence says **letters should accompany sound work**. Shanahan's "RIP to Advanced Phonemic Awareness" reaches the same conclusion. Kilpatrick's claim that severely dyslexic students need heavy oral auditory training **remains a hypothesis that has not been directly tested.** This one matters commercially because Heggerty (oral PA, market leader) is in ~classrooms everywhere.
- **Exact phonics dosage and duration** — the 2024 PA meta-analysis found diminishing returns after ~10.2 hours but no "magic number"; whether to taper PA by fall of grade 1 (Peltier) or run it longer (Heggerty's 8–12 min through grade 1) is unsettled.
- **Sequencing** — must PA precede phonics, or be integrated with it? Contested.
- **Decodable-text dosage and the transition point** to authentic text (Chapter 4).
- **How much of comprehension is teachable strategy vs. a byproduct of background knowledge** (the Hirsch/knowledge-building argument) — real, but weaker-evidenced than the code side.

The defensible primer stance: **the code-based core is settled and was the thing balanced literacy got wrong; the meaning-and-comprehension side and several movement add-ons (advanced PA, specific brands, exact dosages) are genuinely open, and a principal who presents the open questions as "settled" will get burned when the next meta-analysis lands.**

- **DIBELS 8th Edition *measures* are free** from the University of Oregon — the assessment itself costs nothing; only Amplify's data-management platform is paid. Many districts believe they must license the whole thing. `previously_missed`

- **UFLI Foundations toolbox** (University of Florida) — the free lesson-resource set with an explicit scope and sequence is usable **even without buying the manual**. `previously_missed`
- **NCII tools charts** (academic screening + progress monitoring) are **free** and are the neutral, vendor-independent way to compare screeners on classification accuracy — a far better basis than vendor marketing. (Verified in R4.) `previously_missed`
- **The Ehri NRP phonics meta-analysis has a free full-text copy** at the UK government education repository (dera.ioe.ac.uk) even though the SAGE journal version is paywalled. The *technical numbers* behind "the science" are free. `previously_missed`
- **State Science-of-Reading hubs are underused free troves** — Massachusetts *Mass Literacy*, Arizona's Science of Reading site, Connecticut's Center for Literacy Research all publish free Simple-View/Rope/MTSS explainers and implementation tools any district can lift. `previously_missed`

Counter-flags (assumed free or proven, is not):
- **Ehri's *phonemic awareness* NRP meta-analysis (RRQ 2001)** is **Wiley-paywalled**; use the open secondary records for the d=0.86/0.53/0.59 figures.

- **"OG-certified" / "multisensory" commands a price premium** for a feature (multisensory) that the direct-comparison evidence (Schlesinger & Gray 2017) does not support; the *structured/explicit* core is what carries the evidence, and that is not proprietary.
- **Acadience and aimswebPlus are paid**, whereas DIBELS 8 and easyCBM are free — the CBM you pay for is not necessarily better-classifying than the free one; check the NCII chart.

## 4. Use-cases for a K-12 administrator

Enumerated distinctly, not blurred — early literacy hits a principal in several separate operational places:

1. **Comply with a new state Science-of-Reading law.** Read *your* statute's four levers (three-cueing ban? screening mandate? approved-curriculum list? training mandate?), map each to a concrete obligation and deadline, and note the retention/"read-by-3" gate if your state has one.
2. **Audit and possibly replace a balanced-literacy curriculum.** Identify whether your K-2 program is three-cueing-based (Units of Study, Fountas & Pinnell guided reading, running records with MSV coding are the tells), then screen replacements through EdReports' K-2 *foundational skills* reviews (R3) and your state's approved list. Keep the read-alouds and knowledge-building; drop the guessing.
3. **Choose or defend a universal/dyslexia screener.** Use the free NCII screening chart, decide your district's sensitivity-vs-specificity tolerance deliberately, and make sure a positive screen routes to diagnostic follow-up, not to a label — because most dyslexia-screening laws blur screen and diagnosis.
4. **Build the reading MTSS content, not just the structure.** Put the WWC PG#21 "Strong" recommendations (phonological awareness, decoding) into Tier 1; design Tier 2 to *add to* rather than *replace* Tier 1 core (the Grade-1 negative-impact warning); reserve Tier 3 + DBI for non-responders.
5. **Decide how to invest in teacher knowledge.** Weigh LETRS (expensive, mandated in some states, efficacy not yet causally proven) against cheaper knowledge-building (FCRR courses, Reading League, UFLI) — the mechanism that matters (teachers understanding the code) is not proprietary.
6. **Set a decodable-text policy for K-1.** Use decodables during the initial phonics window as a deliberate temporary scaffold aligned to your phonics scope-and-sequence; don't mandate them as a permanent diet, and don't buy the claim they're proven to raise long-run achievement.
7. **Handle a "Sold a Story" parent or board member.** Have the honest map (Chapter 9): the code-based core is settled and balanced literacy got it wrong; the comprehension side and several add-ons are open. Neither dismiss the parent nor over-promise a silver bullet.
8. **Screen a vendor's "aligned to the Science of Reading" claim.** Ask which of the Big Five it actually teaches explicitly and systematically, whether it uses decodables, whether it contains any three-cueing/MSV prompting, and — for intervention products — which ESSA tier and NCII rating (route the efficacy-tier question to R3's WWC/ESSA machinery).
9. **Set a read-by-3 / retention process (if your state mandates one).** Bundle retention with *intensive remediation* (the Florida evidence attaches whatever benefit exists to the remediation, not the holding-back alone), apply good-cause exemptions correctly (ELs, IEPs — route to `education-law`), and communicate to families that the evidence is mixed.
10. **Distinguish the two poor-reader profiles the Simple View predicts.** Make sure screening and intervention address *language comprehension* poor readers (the grade-2 fluent decoders who collapse in grade 4), not only decoding — a district that screens decoding only is half-blind.
11. **Communicate NAEP reality to a community.** ~40% of 4th graders below NAEP Basic and reading down 5 points since 2019 is the backdrop; frame reform as a long-horizon foundational-skills investment, not a one-year test-score play.

## 5. Cross-links

**`college-course-design-and-pedagogy` — already covered; do NOT repeat.** Backward design, constructive alignment, UDL at college level. There is essentially **no overlap** here: early literacy is a K-3 developmental-skill domain with no college analogue. The one seam worth a sentence: the *explicit-instruction* principle that structured literacy embodies is the K-3 instance of the same direct-instruction evidence that R4 and the learning-science primer carry at other levels.

**`learning-science-foundations` — owns the mechanisms; cite, don't restate.** Orthographic mapping and the move from effortful decoding to automatic word recognition **is** cognitive-load-theory and retrieval/automaticity in a specific domain; the Rope's "increasingly automatic" strands are automaticity theory made curricular. This primer supplies the **domain-specific instantiation and the field effect sizes** (NRP d-values, WWC ratings); the theory primer supplies *why* automaticity frees working memory for comprehension. Do not re-derive cognitive load here.

**`applied-behavior-analysis` — already covered.** PBIS/FBA/BIP and behavioral MTSS are R4/ABA territory. This primer's MTSS is the **academic-reading** tier system only; where a reading-intervention delivery problem is actually a behavior problem, route there.

**`school-counseling-practice` — already covered.** The counselor-side of MTSS, mandated reporting, ASCA. No overlap with reading instruction.

**`testing-and-credentialing-policy` — owns psychometrics; hand over at the score-interpretation boundary.** Screener *classification accuracy* (sensitivity/specificity/AUC), the validity of a dyslexia-screen inference, and CBM reliability theory belong there; this primer stops at "use the NCII chart to pick a screener and route positives to diagnosis." The technical validity of a dyslexia *diagnosis* is theirs.

**`education-law` — owns the entitlements.** Dyslexia identification under IDEA, whether a specific program (OG) can be *required* on an IEP, retention/promotion legal exposure, and good-cause-exemption compliance all route there. This primer states only the instructional-quality facts (structured/explicit instruction is what carries the evidence; OG-brand and multisensory specifically do not).

**R3 (`curriculum-standards-and-assessment`) — the adjacent K-12 file.** Early-literacy curriculum adoption *is* an HQIM adoption — EdReports now reviews K-2 foundational-skills materials, and state SoR laws are frequently HQIM-approved-list laws wearing a reading label. Use R3's EdReports/WWC/ESSA machinery for the *materials-quality and efficacy-tier* questions; use this file for the *reading-content* questions (Big Five, decodables, three-cueing).

**Suggested new adjacency, if the codex lacks it:** a `content-knowledge-and-comprehension` seam. The comprehension/background-knowledge argument (Hirsch, knowledge-building curricula, the weak-evidenced "meaning" side of the Rope) is the least-settled and least-covered part of early literacy, and it sits awkwardly between this file's code-focus and R3's curriculum-focus — it currently has no clean home.
