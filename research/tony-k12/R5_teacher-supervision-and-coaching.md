## 2. Findings — candidate chapters

### Chapter 1 — Clinical supervision: the cycle every observation protocol still descends from

The pre-conference / observation / post-conference cycle that structures nearly every US teacher-observation system is not a modern invention — it is **clinical supervision**, developed at Harvard by **Morris Cogan and Robert Goldhammer** in the 1960s and published in Goldhammer's *Clinical Supervision* (1969). Goldhammer's model is **five stages**: (1) a pre-observation conference between supervisor and teacher on the lesson to be observed; (2) the classroom observation; (3) the supervisor's analysis of the observation data and planning for the conference; (4) the post-observation conference; (5) the supervisor's analysis of that conference (a self-review of the supervision itself). The **linchpin** — and the part that survived into Danielson, Marzano and every district framework — is the three-part core: **pre-conference → observation → post-conference**.

Two design commitments in the original are worth carrying because districts routinely lose them. First, Cogan insisted on a **collegial, non-judgmental** relationship: the teacher is an active partner who helps set the observation focus, not a subject being inspected. Second, the pre-conference is the **cornerstone** — it establishes the focus and determines which data-collection tools will yield useful evidence, so the post-conference can be about pre-agreed questions rather than the supervisor's roving impressions. When modern evaluation strips the pre-conference (as "walkthrough" and drive-by models do), it is not streamlining clinical supervision; it is discarding the mechanism that made it developmental.

The field then split. **Developmental / differentiated supervision** (Glickman and others) argued that a single supervisory style fits no one — novices, struggling veterans, and self-directed experts need *directive*, *collaborative*, and *non-directive* stances respectively. This is the intellectual root of today's "differentiated evaluation" (a light-touch cycle for tenured teachers in good standing, an intensive cycle for those on improvement plans).

**Evidence grade: weak/descriptive.** Clinical supervision is a *design tradition*, not an empirically validated intervention. There is essentially no rigorous causal literature showing that "the clinical supervision cycle" as such raises teacher effectiveness or student achievement; its endurance rests on face validity and its incorporation into frameworks that were themselves later studied. The honest framing for a principal: the cycle is the sound *container*, but what fills it (the quality of the framework, the feedback, and whether coaching is separated from judgment — Chapters 7–9) is where any effect lives.

### Chapter 2 — The two observation frameworks a US principal actually uses: Danielson and Marzano

**Danielson's Framework for Teaching (FFT)** is the dominant instrument. Its architecture is **4 domains / 22 components**: Domain 1 Planning & Preparation, Domain 2 Classroom Environment, Domain 3 Instruction, Domain 4 Professional Responsibilities. Domains 1 and 4 are largely *off-stage* (they are evidenced through planning documents and professional conduct, not the observed lesson), while Domains 2 and 3 are what an observer scores in the room. The **2022 revision** is now the operative version — districts using Danielson were required to move to the 2022 FFT for 2025–26 evaluations — and it re-centers equity, cultural responsiveness, and student agency in the language of the components. Danielson also distilled the 22 components into **6 Clusters** for professional-growth use (not scoring): (1) Clarity of Instructional Purpose and Accuracy of Content; (2) A Safe, Respectful, Supportive, and Challenging Learning Environment; (3) Classroom Management; (4) Student Intellectual Engagement; (5) Successful Learning by All Students; (6) Professionalism. The clusters exist because 22 components is too many to hold in a coaching conversation; the clusters are the "big ideas."

**Marzano's Focused Teacher Evaluation Model (FTEM)** is the main competitor: **4 domains / 23 elements** — Standards-Based Planning (3), Standards-Based Instruction (10), Conditions for Learning (7), Professional Responsibilities (3). The design bias is different from Danielson's: Marzano front-loads **instruction** (10 of 23 elements) and ties elements to a "focused" set of high-probability strategies, delivered on the **iObservation** platform. Marzano Center reports its model rests on **277,000+ classroom observations (2012–2018)** and cites Basileo & Toth (2019), a Florida study of 12,000+ teachers reporting that the **observation score was the strongest predictor of student growth** — active in ~30 states.

**Evidence grade on frameworks: real but modest, and vendor-inflated.** Two honest cautions. (a) The Basileo & Toth validity claim is a **vendor-affiliated study** and should be labeled as such; the independent MET finding (Chapter 3) is that observation scores *do* predict student growth but **weakly and with substantial measurement error**, and that no single measure predicts performance on conceptually demanding tasks well. (b) A framework's rubric quality is not the binding constraint on evaluation quality — **rater behavior is** (Chapter 5). A principal choosing between Danielson and Marzano is choosing a vocabulary and a platform, not buying an effect size; the effect, such as it is, comes from what observers *do* with the instrument. Flag the vendor-vs-independent evidence gap explicitly.

### Chapter 3 — Value-added models, the MET study, and the statistical controversy

**Value-added models (VAM)** estimate a teacher's contribution to student test-score growth by controlling statistically for prior achievement and student characteristics. They became the fulcrum of a decade of policy because they promised an *objective* counterweight to inflated observation ratings.

**The Gates MET (Measures of Effective Teaching) study** (2009–2013, ~3,000 teachers, six districts) is the most-cited empirical anchor. Its headline conclusion: great teaching can be identified by **combining three measures — classroom observations, student surveys (Tripod), and student achievement gains (VAM)** — and no single measure alone is adequate. MET's practical recommendations: weight the composite so VAM is roughly a third to half (its own analyses favored a balanced composite that was *less* volatile year-to-year than VAM alone), include student surveys, and — critically for reliability — **average observations across more than one observer and more than one lesson**; a single administrator on a single lesson is not reliable. The honest caveat that MET's own critics (and its supplemental-assessment data) surfaced: **none of the three measures predicted well onto "conceptually demanding" tasks** — they predict state-test growth better than they predict deeper learning.

**The controversy.** The **American Statistical Association's 2014 statement** is the single most important citation here, and it is devastating in a quiet way: teachers account for **only about 1% to 14% of the variability in student test scores**; the majority of the variation is system-level. The ASA warned that **ranking teachers by VAM can have unintended consequences that reduce quality**, that VAM estimates are **unstable across years, classes, and statistical models**, and that VAM should be used **within a quality-improvement frame, not as a standalone high-stakes instrument**. The AERA issued a parallel 2015 caution.

**The strongest pro-VAM evidence** is Chetty, Friedman & Rockoff's *Measuring the Impacts of Teachers* (I and II, *American Economic Review* 2014) — 1M+ students linked to tax records. They found VAM estimates show **little forecast bias** (a quasi-experimental test using teacher switches), and that students assigned to **high-VA teachers are more likely to attend college, earn more, and less likely to have teen births**; replacing a bottom-5% teacher with an average one is worth ≈ **$250,000 in class lifetime earnings**. This is real and important — but note what it does and does not say: it validates VAM as a *predictor of long-run outcomes on average across many teachers*, which is a different claim from *VAM is reliable enough to rank and fire an individual teacher this year*. The reconciliation the evidence supports: **VAM carries real signal in aggregate and is dangerous as an individual high-stakes instrument** because of instability and the 1–14% ceiling. Both the reformers who treated VAM as decisive and the opponents who called it noise overstated.

### Chapter 4 — The policy arc: Widget Effect → Race to the Top → the post-ESSA retreat

This is the chapter that explains why a 2026 principal's evaluation system looks the way it does, and why it is quietly reverting.

**The Widget Effect (TNTP, 2009)** is the founding indictment. Across 4 states, 12 districts, ~15,000 teachers and ~1,300 administrators, it documented that **fewer than 1% of teachers were rated unsatisfactory**, that evaluation treated teachers as interchangeable "widgets," and that **roughly 3 in 4 teachers received no specific feedback** to improve. Binary satisfactory/unsatisfactory ratings made excellence and ineffectiveness equally invisible.

**Race to the Top (2009) and NCLB waivers** turned that indictment into federal leverage: states competing for funds (and later seeking accountability waivers) adopted multiple-measure evaluations with a **required student-growth component**. The scale of the shift is stark — **in 2009 only 15 states required objective growth measures; by 2015, 43 did** (Shanker Institute). Systems like DC's IMPACT became national exemplars.

**The retreat.** The **2015 Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA)** removed the federal push, returning teacher evaluation to states. What followed was a broad walk-back: states dropped or de-weighted VAM/student-growth, several dropped student surveys, and some (notably **DC and Kentucky**, with Arkansas, Oklahoma, Wyoming close behind) made wholesale retreats. NCTQ's State-of-the-States tracking documents the reversal.

**The verdict on the whole reform wave** is now in, and it is the sharpest single fact in this sub-area: **Bleiberg, Brunner, Harbatkin, Kraft & Springer (NBER w30995, 2023)** estimated the national effect of the teacher-evaluation reforms on student achievement and attainment and found **precisely estimated null effects** — ruling out effects as small as **0.015 SD** for achievement and **1 percentage point** for graduation and college enrollment. A decade of high-stakes reform, at national scale, produced essentially nothing on student outcomes. The authors' five candidate explanations: political opposition, decentralized implementation, insufficient capacity, limited generalizability of the evaluation *designs*, and the absence of compensation increases to offset the reduced job security teachers experienced. Kraft's *Revisiting the Widget Effect* (2016) adds the mechanism: even after reform, **ratings re-compressed at the top** — the distributions widened only slightly.

**Evidence grade: strong (this is well-identified).** The honest lesson for a principal is not "evaluation doesn't matter" but "**accountability-first, test-based evaluation at scale did not move outcomes; the money and political capital would have bought more as investment in development.**" This is the empirical spine of the growth-vs-accountability argument in Chapter 13.

### Chapter 5 — Why observation scores are less reliable than they look: rater bias

A framework is only as good as the human applying it, and the human is a biased instrument. The rater-effects literature is consistent:

- **Leniency** is pervasive: principals overuse the top rating categories — the same Widget Effect compression, now measured psychometrically. A Many-Facet Rasch study of **421 principals** found principals generally *did* differentiate more- from less-effective teachers, but rated with **systematic leniency**, and about **12% exhibited severe rater bias**.
- **Halo effect** (letting a global impression of the teacher color every component score) and **central tendency** are the other two classic errors. Encouragingly, the same study found **minimal halo** when principals used a well-anchored instrument — suggesting the rubric design matters.
- **Rater training and instrument type both help.** Behaviorally anchored rating scales (BARS) produce **less leniency and halo and higher inter-rater reliability** than graphic/Likert scales, and **training reduces both errors**. This is exactly why MET recommended multiple observers and multiple lessons, and why serious systems require observer **calibration and certification**.

**The practical translation** (well-supported): a single principal, untrained, scoring a single lesson on a vague rubric produces a number dominated by leniency and halo. Reliability is bought three ways — (1) a well-anchored instrument, (2) trained/calibrated observers, (3) **multiple observations by multiple observers**. A principal who cannot afford multiple trained observers should treat a lone observation score as *formative signal*, not a defensible summative rating — a point that routes directly into due-process risk if a dismissal rests on it (see `education-law`).

### Chapter 6 — Walkthroughs and learning walks: the counter-intuitive evidence

Brief, frequent classroom **walkthroughs** ("learning walks," "focus walks," Downey's 3-minute walkthrough) are among the most popular instructional-leadership practices — and the evidence on them is genuinely uncomfortable.

The key study is **Grissom, Loeb & Master (2013, *Educational Researcher*)** — full-day in-person observations of ~**100 urban principals over 3 years**, linked to achievement gains. The findings: time spent on **teacher coaching, evaluation, and developing the instructional program predicts positive achievement gains**, but time spent on **informal classroom walkthroughs negatively predicts student growth — particularly in high schools**. Broad "time on instruction" did not predict gains at all; **specificity and quality of the activity mattered, not aggregate instructional time**. The negative association was larger where walkthroughs were *not* experienced as professional-development opportunities.

**How to read this honestly** (the study is correlational/observational, so causality is not proven): the defensible interpretation is *not* "walkthroughs cause lower achievement." It is that **walkthroughs as commonly practiced — high-frequency, low-depth, checklist drive-bys with little follow-up feedback — are a low-yield use of scarce principal time**, and may be a marker of a principal substituting visible activity for the harder, higher-yield work of sustained coaching and program development. The design fix the broader literature supports: walkthroughs work only when everyone knows the **focus and purpose**, the "look-fors" are pre-agreed, and each walk produces **specific feedback** — i.e., when a walkthrough is re-attached to the clinical-supervision logic (Chapter 1) it was severed from. Flag this as **contested and easily over-read**: it is one strong observational study, not an RCT, but it is the best evidence available and it cuts against a near-universal practice.

### Chapter 7 — Instructional coaching: the strongest positive intervention in this whole file, and its scale problem

If Chapters 3–4 are the disappointing news about evaluation, coaching is the hopeful news — with a large asterisk.

**The evidence.** Kraft, Blazar & Hogan's meta-analysis (*Review of Educational Research*, 2018) pooled **60 causal studies** of teacher coaching and found **+0.49 SD on instruction and +0.18 SD on student achievement** — large effects by education-research standards, and among the strongest for any teacher-development intervention. Two moderators are decisive for a principal:

1. **Coaching paired with group training** outperforms either alone.
2. **More hours did not reliably mean more impact** — quality and focus beat dosage.
3. **The scale problem — the single most important caveat:** effects were **substantially smaller in large programs than in small ones**. The strong average is driven heavily by tightly-run, well-resourced, often literacy-focused elementary/pre-K programs. When districts scale coaching across hundreds of teachers with thinly-trained coaches, the effect shrinks toward the low end. Do **not** promise a district the 0.18 SD from a 20-teacher pilot.

**The named models a principal will encounter:**
- **Jim Knight's Impact Cycle / Instructional Coaching (partnership model).** A 3-stage cycle — **Identify** (a clear goal from a clear picture of current reality; goals are "PEERS": Powerful, Easy, Emotionally compelling, Reachable, Student-focused), **Learn** (coach explains/models a strategy), **Improve** (iterate to the goal). It rests on **seven partnership principles** — equality, choice, voice, dialogue, reflection, praxis, reciprocity — framing coaching as a conversation between equals, explicitly **not** evaluation.
- **Cognitive Coaching (Costa & Garmston).** A *non-directive, reflective* model aimed at teacher self-directedness, built on **Five States of Mind** (efficacy, flexibility, craftsmanship, consciousness, interdependence) and three conversation maps: **Planning**, **Reflecting**, and **Problem-Resolving**. The coach mediates thinking rather than supplying answers — the opposite pole from Knight's more strategy-forward stance.
- **Content-Focused Coaching (West & Staub).** Subject-specific (rooted in mathematics); the coach co-designs, co-teaches, and reflects on rigorous standards-based lessons, keeping the content and student thinking — not generic pedagogy — at the center.

**The honest framing:** coaching *works* when it is sustained, focused, paired with training, and — the recurring finding — **content-specific and job-embedded** rather than generic. The three named models are stances on a directive↔non-directive spectrum, not competing efficacy claims; the meta-analysis did not crown a model, it validated the *category* under conditions districts routinely fail to reproduce at scale.

### Chapter 8 — Coaching vs. evaluation: why the roles must be separated

This is the seam where the two halves of the file collide, and the practitioner literature is unusually unanimous.

When the **same person** both coaches and evaluates — or when the coaching visit is also the rating visit — teachers rationally **protect themselves**: they teach safe lessons, treat every drop-in as a test, and withhold the exact struggles coaching exists to surface. The developmental function and the judgment function **poison each other** when they share a source. The consistent recommendations: (1) **coaches should not conduct walk-throughs or evaluations** and should not be assigned administrative-judgment tasks; (2) **coaching should be confidential** — performance scores should not appear on coaching documents, and there should be clear, ideally indirect reporting lines; (3) the relationship should be governed by an explicit **charter** naming goals, boundaries, data ownership, and opt-out conditions.

**Evidence grade: practitioner-consensus and qualitative, not causal.** There is no clean RCT isolating "role separation" as a variable; the claim rests on convergent qualitative research, the trust literature, and the logic of incentives. But it is *consistent*, and it is reinforced by the meta-analytic finding that the strong coaching effects came from programs where coaching was **developmental and non-evaluative**. The single most important structural decision a principal makes about coaching is **who the coach reports to and whether the coach's notes can be used against the teacher** — get that wrong and the +0.49 SD instruction effect does not appear. The tension a small school faces honestly: in a building too small to separate the roles, the principal must at minimum **separate the occasions** (announced coaching cycles vs. evaluation cycles) and be transparent about which hat is on — an imperfect mitigation, not a solution.

### Chapter 9 — Feedback that actually changes practice

Feedback is the payload of the whole observation-and-coaching apparatus, and its evidence is more sobering than PD decks suggest.

The best current synthesis is **Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), *The Power of Feedback Revisited*** — 435 studies, 994 effect sizes, 61,000+ participants — finding an overall **d = 0.48**, but with **enormous heterogeneity**: feedback "cannot be understood as a single consistent treatment." The moderator that matters is **information content** — high-information feedback (what specifically to do differently, tied to a goal) far outperforms low-information feedback (praise, grades, vague "great job / needs work"). Feedback also moves **cognitive and skill outcomes more than motivational/behavioral** ones. Note this meta-analysis is mostly *student* feedback; the read-across to teacher feedback is a reasonable inference, not a direct finding — flag it.

Translated to a post-observation conference (well-supported by the coaching and feedback literatures jointly): feedback changes practice when it is **specific, actionable, focused on one or two things, tied to a goal the teacher owns, and followed by a chance to try again** — which is precisely the Impact-Cycle / clinical-supervision loop. Feedback fails when it is a comprehensive 22-component data dump (cognitive overload), when it is evaluative rather than developmental (Chapter 8), or when there is no follow-up cycle to act on it (the Widget Effect's "no specific feedback" failure). The number of components a principal *scores* and the number they *give feedback on* should be different by an order of magnitude.

### Chapter 10 — Professional development that works: job-embedded and sustained vs. the one-shot workshop

The empirical divide here is old and robust. **One-time, stand-and-deliver workshops have limited-to-no measurable effect** on teacher practice or student outcomes; the effective form is **sustained, intensive, collaborative, job-embedded, content-focused, and classroom-applied** — the language ESSA itself wrote into its definition of professional learning (which explicitly excludes "stand-alone, 1-day, or short-term workshops").

The anchor citation is **Yoon et al. (2007, REL Southwest)** — and its honest headline is a *scarcity* finding as much as an effect finding: of **1,300+ studies**, only **9** met WWC evidence standards. Across those nine, average effect on achievement was **~0.54 SD**, and the studies averaged **~49 contact hours** of PD; the widely-quoted translation is that ~49 hours of sustained PD was associated with a **~21-percentile-point** student gain, while under ~14 hours showed no statistically significant effect. Two cautions must ride with that number: (a) it rests on only nine studies — a thin base carrying an enormous amount of citation weight; (b) "49 hours" is a descriptive average, **not** a validated threshold — the mechanism is *sustained and applied*, not a magic hour count. More recent syntheses (e.g., the 2015 Kennedy review) complicate the "structural features" story further, finding that **what** the PD teaches matters more than its format.

**Learning Forward's Standards for Professional Learning (2022)** are the field's normative framework, and the **AIR meta-analysis (2021)** reports that PD aligned to those standards is associated with improved instruction and, downstream, achievement. The undergirding theory is **andragogy** (next paragraph). Evidence grade: the *format* findings (sustained > one-shot) are strong and convergent; the *specific numbers* (49 hours, 0.54 SD) rest on a thin base and should be cited as illustrative, not load-bearing.

**Adult learning theory (andragogy, Malcolm Knowles)** is the reason job-embedded PD works, and it must be reported with its critique attached. Knowles' assumptions: adults are **self-directed**, bring **rich prior experience**, are **ready to learn** what is immediately relevant, are **problem-centered** rather than content-centered, are **internally motivated**, and **need to know why** before engaging. His four principles: involve adults in planning, build on their experience, ensure immediate relevance, and center problem-solving. **The critique is essential and often omitted:** andragogy's assumptions were **not empirically derived** — they came from Knowles' observation and theory, and much of it applies to motivated children too. Knowles himself later recast it as a **spectrum** between teacher-directed and self-directed, situation-dependent. So andragogy is a **useful design heuristic with weak empirical standing**, not a validated theory; present it as the "why" behind respecting teacher expertise and relevance, not as proven science.

### Chapter 11 — PLCs: the collaboration structure, and its softer-than-advertised evidence

**Professional Learning Communities (DuFour, DuFour, Eaker)** are the dominant teacher-collaboration structure. DuFour's PLC is defined as *"an ongoing process in which educators work collaboratively in recurring cycles of collective inquiry and action research to achieve better results for the students they serve."* The operating engine is **four questions**: (1) What do we want each student to learn? (2) How will we know when each has learned it? (3) How will we respond when a student does not learn it? (4) How will we extend learning for students who already have? DuFour's claim is that **the third question** — a systematic, timely, directive response to students who struggle — is what separates a real PLC from a school that merely calls its meetings "PLCs."

**Evidence grade: promising but fragmented.** This is the honest note the DuFour marketing does not carry: the empirical base for PLCs is **methodologically weak and inconsistent** — the term is used to mean wildly different things, theoretical approaches diverge, and a *definitive* causal evidence base "remains elusive." Where PLCs are associated with gains, it is under conditions of **genuine implementation** (shared results-orientation, common formative assessment, collective responsibility) — which is exactly the variable that is hardest to standardize and easiest to fake. The practical stance for a principal: PLCs are a **sound organizing structure whose effect depends entirely on implementation fidelity**, and "we have PLC time on the schedule" is not evidence of a PLC. This mirrors the WWC "Minimal Evidence" finding on the entire data-use apparatus (already in the curriculum/assessment field — cross-link, don't repeat).

### Chapter 12 — Induction and mentoring: the highest-leverage move for novices and for retention

The clearest positive in the *novice*-teacher space is **induction and mentoring**. **Ingersoll & Strong's critical review (*Review of Educational Research*, 2011)** synthesized **15 empirical studies** and found that beginning teachers who received induction/mentoring showed positive impacts on **three outcomes**: **commitment and retention**, **classroom instructional practice** (staying on task, questioning, adjusting to student interest, classroom management), and **student achievement** (almost all studies showed higher scores/gains).

**The essential caveat, which Ingersoll & Strong report themselves:** the **one large randomized controlled trial** in the set (the IES study of comprehensive induction in urban, low-income schools) found **some positive effects on student achievement but no effects on retention or classroom practice** — the opposite pattern from the weaker observational studies. So the strongest single design in the literature is the least confirmatory on the outcomes districts most want (retention). The defensible synthesis: induction/mentoring is **well-supported and low-risk**, likely the best per-dollar move for novice development and retention, but the retention effect specifically is **weaker and more design-dependent** than advocates claim, and *comprehensive, sustained, well-matched* mentoring — not a nominal "assigned a buddy" program — is what the positive studies actually tested. Dosage and quality of the mentor match are the variables; a mentor with no release time and no training is the null condition.

### Chapter 13 — The growth-vs-accountability tension, and formative vs. summative evaluation

Everything above resolves into one structural tension a principal manages daily: **teacher evaluation is asked to do two incompatible jobs at once** — to **develop** teachers (formative, low-stakes, trust-dependent, confidential) and to **hold them accountable** (summative, high-stakes, comparative, on the record). The evidence says these jobs pull in opposite directions: the accountability apparatus, at national scale, moved no outcomes (Chapter 4), while the developmental apparatus (coaching, induction, sustained PD) shows the real effects (Chapters 7, 10, 12) — but only when it is **kept separate from judgment** (Chapter 8).

**Formative vs. summative evaluation** is the vocabulary for managing this:
- **Formative** — ongoing, developmental, feedback-rich, ideally confidential, aimed at improvement; this is where coaching, walkthroughs-done-right, and the clinical cycle live.
- **Summative** — periodic, on-the-record, tied to employment consequences (tenure, dismissal, sometimes pay); this is where the framework rating, the multiple-observer requirement, and due-process documentation live (dismissal procedure → `education-law`).

The practical, evidence-consistent design a principal can defend: run a **differentiated system** — a light, growth-oriented, formative cycle for the large majority of effective tenured teachers (protecting the trust that makes development work), and an intensive, well-documented summative cycle reserved for **genuine underperformance** (where reliability, multiple observers, and legal defensibility become paramount). Trying to run every teacher through a maximal high-stakes cycle every year is the design that the Widget-Effect-to-ESSA arc showed produces compressed ratings, no feedback, and no outcome gains — while consuming the principal time the Grissom study says is better spent coaching. The tension cannot be *resolved*; it can only be *structured* so the two functions stop poisoning each other.

## 4. Use-cases for a K-12 administrator

Enumerated distinctly, not blurred:

1. **Run a standard observation cycle.** Execute the clinical loop with fidelity: a real **pre-conference** to set focus and data tools, a focused observation, and a post-conference built on the pre-agreed focus — not a drive-by plus a rubric dump. The pre-conference is the load-bearing stage most systems skip.
2. **Choose or operate an observation framework.** Understand Danielson (4 domains/22 components, 2022 version, 6 clusters for coaching) vs. Marzano (4 domains/23 elements, instruction-heavy, iObservation). Choose on fit and platform, not on vendor effect-size claims; hold Domains 1 and 4 to evidence outside the lesson.
3. **Make an observation score defensible.** Before any score carries employment weight, ensure a well-anchored instrument, a **trained/calibrated** observer, and **multiple observations by multiple observers**. A lone untrained single-lesson score is formative signal only — resting a dismissal on it is a due-process risk (→ `education-law`).
4. **Decide how much weight to give test-based / VAM measures.** Know the ASA ceiling (teachers = 1–14% of score variance) and VAM's year-to-year instability; use growth data as *one* signal in a composite (MET's balanced-composite logic), never as a standalone individual verdict. Be able to cite both Chetty (aggregate signal is real) and the ASA (individual ranking is dangerous).
5. **Explain to a board or union why the evaluation system changed (again).** Tell the honest arc: Widget Effect → Race to the Top mandates → ESSA retreat → the NBER null-at-scale finding. This is why de-weighting high-stakes VAM in favor of development is evidence-based, not a retreat from rigor.
6. **Decide whether to keep, cut, or refocus walkthroughs.** Know the Grissom finding (frequent informal walkthroughs negatively predict growth; coaching/evaluation/program-development predict gains). If keeping walkthroughs, re-attach them to a pre-agreed focus and mandatory specific feedback, or redirect that time to coaching.
7. **Stand up or fix an instructional-coaching program.** Expect the meta-analytic effects (+0.49 instruction, +0.18 achievement) **only** under the conditions that produced them — sustained, content-focused, paired with group training, and small/well-resourced. Do not promise pilot-scale effects at district scale.
8. **Separate coaching from evaluation — the single highest-leverage structural decision.** Ensure coaches don't evaluate or do walk-throughs, coaching notes stay confidential and off the personnel record, and reporting lines protect the developmental relationship. In a school too small to separate people, separate the *occasions* and be transparent about which hat is on.
9. **Match a coaching model to the need.** Directive/strategy-forward (Knight's Impact Cycle) for concrete skill gaps and novices; non-directive/reflective (Cognitive Coaching) for self-directed veterans; content-focused (West & Staub) when the gap is subject-specific rigor.
10. **Deliver a post-observation conference that changes practice.** One or two specific, actionable, goal-tied items with a follow-up cycle — not a 22-component data dump. Score comprehensively; give feedback narrowly.
11. **Design professional development that isn't wasted.** Choose sustained, job-embedded, content-focused PD over one-shot workshops; use the ~49-hour Yoon figure as an *illustration* of "sustained," not a threshold; align to Learning Forward standards; respect andragogy as a design heuristic (with its weak empirical standing acknowledged).
12. **Build a functioning PLC, not PLC-labeled meeting time.** Drive the four DuFour questions, especially the systematic response to students who haven't learned; recognize that scheduled collaboration time is a container whose effect depends entirely on implementation fidelity.
13. **Induct and mentor novices well.** Invest in comprehensive, sustained mentoring with released, trained mentors and good matches — the best per-dollar novice-development and retention move — while knowing the retention effect specifically is weaker and more design-dependent than advocates claim.
14. **Run a differentiated (formative vs. summative) system.** A light developmental cycle for the effective majority (protecting the trust development needs); an intensive, well-documented summative cycle reserved for genuine underperformance. Don't maximize stakes for everyone — that is the design the evidence shows fails.
15. **Support a struggling teacher toward improvement or exit.** Combine an intensive, documented summative cycle (reliability, multiple observers, a defensible record) with genuinely separate developmental support — the growth path and the accountability path run in parallel, not merged, with the dismissal machinery itself routed to `education-law`.
