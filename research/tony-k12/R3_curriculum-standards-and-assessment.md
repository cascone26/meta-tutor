## 2. Findings — candidate chapters

### Chapter 1 — How K-12 standards actually work, and what standards alone buy you

Standards in the US are a **state** instrument, not a federal one, and the layer people argue about (Common Core) sits on top of a much older machinery of state content frameworks, state adoption cycles, and state assessment blueprints. 46 states initially adopted CCSS. The subsequent history is mostly **rebranding, not reversal**: full withdrawals are a short list (Indiana first, March 2014; Oklahoma and South Carolina June 2014; Arizona October 2015; Kentucky 2017; Tennessee phased out 2016), while a much larger group renamed and lightly edited — Georgia's "Standards of Excellence," Florida's "B.E.S.T.," Alabama's "College and Career Ready Standards." Never-adopters: Texas, Virginia, Nebraska, Alaska (substantially similar), Minnesota (ELA only). Practically, a K-12 administrator in 2026 is running a curriculum that is CCSS-descended even where the word is banned.

NGSS is structurally different and worth teaching as the contrast case: it is not a list of content, it is **performance expectations built from three dimensions** (science & engineering practices × disciplinary core ideas × crosscutting concepts), derived from the NRC *Framework for K-12 Science Education*, developed by 26 lead states with a 40-member writing team. The consequence for an administrator is concrete — three-dimensional standards cannot be assessed with a content checklist, which is why NGSS shipped its own EQuIP Rubric and Lesson Screener.

**Evidence grade on standards-as-lever: weak to null, and contested.** Bleiberg's AERA Open diff-in-diff on NAEP (free full text, ERIC EJ1323929) is the most careful estimate available and it splits: a large positive initial effect for economically advantaged students, **no detectable initial effect for economically disadvantaged students**. Loveless's reading of the literature is that achievement is roughly where it would have been without CCSS. Song/Yang/Garet found a negative sign. The defensible statement for the primer: **the sign is unsettled and the magnitude is small; raising the written expectation without changing materials, time and support is not an intervention.** Flag this as contested — the pro-standards and anti-standards literatures both overstate.

### Chapter 2 — Curriculum as a policy lever: the strongest claim in K-12 reform, and its strongest counterexample

The HQIM movement rests on Chingos & Whitehurst's *Choosing Blindly* (Brookings 2012), which argued that materials effects rival teacher effects while nobody even records which materials are in use. Downstream, the movement's headline numbers are: switching to a top-quartile textbook ≈ **0.127 student-level SD ≈ 3.6 percentile points**; effect equivalent to moving an average teacher to the 80th percentile; roughly **40× more cost-effective than class-size reduction**, because textbooks cost about the same regardless of quality — the marginal cost of choosing better is near zero.

The honest counterweight, and the single most important thing this chapter must carry: **Kane and Blazar's *Learning by the Book* (CEPR, 2019) found essentially no difference in average 4th/5th grade math achievement gains across schools using different textbooks**, pooling six states (CA, LA, MD, NJ, NM, WA) over 2014-15 to 2016-17. The Fordham critique is fair and should be reported alongside rather than used to dismiss: too few schools actually switched textbooks in-window; ideally you want three years on either side of an adoption; teachers reported only a few days of PD and were supplementing with material found online; and post-CCSS textbooks have converged, compressing the very variance the study was trying to detect.

The reconciliation that the evidence actually supports — and this is the chapter's thesis — is a **conditional** one: curriculum effects appear when curriculum is paired with sustained curriculum-based professional learning, and vanish when materials are dropped into buildings as objects. Short & Hirsh's *The Elements* (Carnegie, 2020) is the named framework; Louisiana is the existence proof (Chapter 3). Note also that the movement's own evidence page (EdReports "Why Materials Matter") presents **no contrary evidence at all** — a primer that repeats it uncritically inherits that bias.

Effect-size calibration for a district: Evidence for ESSA's aggregate for Imagine Learning Illustrative Mathematics is **+0.11 average across 4 studies, n=8,720**, range +0.04 to +0.42, rated "moderate" (ESSA Tier 2). That is the realistic order of magnitude for a well-implemented curriculum swap — real, worth doing, not transformational on its own.

### Chapter 3 — How materials get rated: EdReports, state reviews, and what a rating does not tell you

**EdReports' architecture** (free, no login): each subject-grade band has a Review Criteria document (indicators) plus an Evidence Guide (how to collect evidence, discussion prompts, scoring). Educator reviewers score indicators from evidence in the materials; indicator scores roll up to criteria, criteria to gateways, by a stated rule-based method. Gateways are **sequential and eliminating**: Gateway 1 and 2 are alignment (for math: focus, coherence, rigor, mathematical practices), Gateway 3 is usability. Materials that fail Gateway 1 never reach Gateway 2; only materials meeting both alignment gateways are reviewed for usability. Some gateways carry minimum scores on designated **non-negotiable** components. Failing to advance yields "not eligible." Coverage now spans ELA (K-12 plus K-2 foundational skills), math, science, Pre-K, multilingual-learner supports, and — newer and relevant to Chapter 6 — **interim assessments in ELA and math, grades 3-8**.

**State reviews** are the parallel and in some states the more consequential system. Louisiana runs its own tiered online reviews (Tier 1 = exemplifies quality, meets all rubric criteria), publishes rubrics in Word and PDF, opens materials to **four weeks of public comment including parents**, and posts annotated reviews publicly and free. The 2026-27 cycle covers early childhood, K-12 science, K-12 social studies, dyslexia intervention, and Tier 1 resubmissions in ELA/math/intensive intervention. The Bellwether case study's key structural finding: Louisiana **avoided a mandate** — reforms were administrative first and codified into law years later — which is why it survived local-control politics. CCSSO estimates 99% of Louisiana middle/high students now have HQIM access.

What a rating does not tell you, and must be said plainly: a green rating is an **alignment and usability** judgment made by reading the materials, not an efficacy finding. It is a different evidentiary object from an ESSA tier. WWC's ESSA mapping requires a study meeting WWC standards, statistically significant positive effects, **≥350 students and ≥2 educational sites** for Tier 1 or 2 — plus population/setting overlap (Tier 1 needs setting *and* sample match to your context; Tier 2 needs setting *or* sample). A district that treats "all-green on EdReports" as "proven effective here" has conflated the two.

### Chapter 4 — Written, taught, tested: coherence, scope and sequence, and pacing guides

The clean vocabulary is IEA/TIMSS's, and it beats the informal American phrasing: **intended curriculum** (system policies, standards, structures — what the system expects taught), **implemented curriculum** (what teachers actually deliver, plus the instructional and home/school conditions), **attained curriculum** (what students learned and how they came to feel about the subject). The 2026 TIMSS Insights report applies this to 8th-grade math and science with TIMSS 2023 data and is free. The administrator-relevant point: the gap between intended and implemented is the real object of curriculum management, and it is measured by **opportunity to learn**, an instrument Schmidt's TIMSS work built — "students cannot learn what they are not taught" is not a slogan, it is an OTL finding.

Schmidt's "**a mile wide and an inch deep**" is the US coherence indictment: US math texts covering 30-35 topics per year against roughly 20 in Germany and 10 in Japan. Coherence in the high-performing systems means fewer topics, systematic within-year and across-year connections, and an expectation of depth. This is where the primer connects to CCSS's own design claim (focus, coherence, rigor) — CCSS was in large part Schmidt's diagnosis turned into policy, which is exactly why the null/small achievement findings in Chapter 1 matter.

TNTP's *The Opportunity Myth* supplies the mechanism at classroom grain: ~4,000 students across five systems spent **over 500 hours a year — roughly six months per core subject — on assignments not at their grade level**, engaged meaningfully less than half the time, and mostly earned A's and B's while graduating unprepared. That is the intended/implemented gap rendered in hours. It also reframes "pacing": the observed failure is not usually teachers running behind the pacing guide, it is grade-level content never being put in front of students.

**Evidence grade on pacing guides specifically: thin.** There is no rigorous causal literature establishing that pacing guides raise achievement; the defensible framing is that scope-and-sequence coherence has strong descriptive/comparative support (TIMSS, OTL) while the pacing-guide-as-compliance-document has essentially none, and TNTP's data suggest the binding constraint is content grade-level, not calendar adherence. Flag as under-evidenced, popular practice outrunning evidence.

### Chapter 5 — Formative assessment: what the effect sizes actually survive

This is the chapter where the primer earns its keep, because the number in circulation is wrong.

**The claim.** Black & Wiliam's *Inside the Black Box* (1998) reported gains in the **0.4–0.7** range from a narrative review, and that band is still quoted in PD decks 28 years later.

**What survived.** Kingston & Nash (2011, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice*) screened **300+ K-12 studies**, found many with designs too flawed to interpret, and could compute effect sizes from only **13**. Random-effects weighted mean: **d = 0.20**. Briggs and colleagues then contested Kingston & Nash's own methodology, so this is a live dispute, not a settled correction. Independent convergence, and this one is open access and current: the 2022 Frontiers meta-analysis of formative assessment on reading achievement — **48 studies, 116,051 K-12 students, d = +0.19 (95% CI 0.15–0.23)**, described by its authors as the smallest estimate among prior meta-analyses on the topic. Publication bias was checked (10 unpublished studies included) and not detected. Its moderators are the actionable part: studies with **≤250 students yielded d=0.33 versus far smaller effects at scale** (the classic small-study inflation pattern); teacher use of **differentiated instruction added +0.13 SD**; and **integrated teacher-and-student assessment beat teacher-only approaches**. Grade level, digital technology, and research design did not moderate.

**Honest bottom line for the primer:** formative assessment is a genuine positive with a defensible effect around **0.2**, not 0.4–0.7, and the part carrying the weight is student involvement plus instructional response — not the frequency of quizzing. Note also that Dylan Wiliam himself has become a public critic of standardized effect sizes as an interpretive currency in education (his objection: effect size is inflated or deflated by the achievement range of the sampled population, so cross-study comparison is often meaningless). Do **not** claim he retracted the 1998 figures — that is not supported by anything I could verify.

**What formative assessment is, definitionally.** The FAST SCASS 2018 revision (verified via the Michigan Assessment Consortium's free copy, since both CCSSO-hosted URLs are dead/blocked): "a planned, ongoing process used by all students and teachers during learning and teaching to elicit and use evidence of student learning to improve student understanding of intended disciplinary learning outcomes and support students to become self-directed learners." Five elements: clarify goals and success criteria within a learning progression; elicit and analyze evidence of student thinking; student self-assessment and peer feedback; actionable feedback; use the evidence to adjust strategies, goals, or next instructional steps. Two things follow that districts routinely get wrong: formative assessment is a **process, not an instrument** — a product cannot be "a formative assessment" — and **students are named as agents** in the definition, which is precisely the moderator the meta-analysis found.

### Chapter 6 — Interim/benchmark assessment and data teams: the widest gap between adoption and evidence

Interim/benchmark testing is close to universal in US districts and its causal evidence is weak and non-replicating. Konstantopoulos and colleagues ran a **staged randomized trial** assigning schools to quarterly interim assessments in K-8 reading and math (Indiana). Cohort 1 showed roughly **0.30 SD in grades 5-6 math** and a smaller effect in grades 3-4 reading — and the effects **did not replicate in cohort 2**. Across the team's later papers the pattern is small, inconsistent, or null; a later analysis found effects in grades 3-8 math roughly **uniform across the achievement distribution** and generally **not narrowing achievement gaps**.

The companion fact, and it is the sharpest single citation in this whole sub-area: **IES/WWC Practice Guide #12, *Using Student Achievement Data to Support Instructional Decision Making*, rates all five of its recommendations at the lowest level — Minimal Evidence.** Those five are: make data part of an ongoing improvement cycle; teach students to examine their own data and set goals; establish a schoolwide vision for data use; provide supports fostering a data-driven culture; build a districtwide data system. So the entire American data-team/PLC/DDDM apparatus rests on expert opinion and practitioner experience, formally acknowledged as such by the federal clearinghouse. That is not a reason to abandon it. It is a reason to stop presenting it as evidence-based and to treat local implementation as the variable that matters.

Note the newer development for the materials-review chapter: EdReports now reviews **interim assessments** in ELA/math grades 3-8 — meaning the quality-review apparatus is being extended from curriculum to the assessment products themselves.

Practical distinctions the chapter must keep separate rather than blur: **formative** (in-the-moment, process, no scores needed), **common formative assessment** (team-built, shared, cycle-level — organizationally useful, evidentially thin as a distinct intervention), **interim/benchmark** (periodic, predictive/evaluative ambitions, weak causal evidence), **summative** (course/unit grade), **large-scale/state** (accountability — belongs to `testing-and-credentialing-policy`).

### Chapter 7 — Grading: where practice, ideology and evidence diverge most

Report the mechanics first, because they are arithmetic and not contestable.

**Zeros and averaging.** The mathematical problem is not the zero, it is the zero **inside an arithmetic mean on a 100-point scale**, where the interval between F and D is 60 points and every other letter interval is 10. One zero can outweigh several passing scores. Guskey's position — no evidence that assigning zeros teaches responsibility, and no research supporting low grades as effective punishment or universal motivator — is defensible **as a negative claim about absence of evidence**, and should be stated that way, not as positive evidence for the alternative. **Minimum grading** (e.g., a floor of 50) is a proposed fix to the scale asymmetry, not an independent pedagogical claim.

**Standards-based grading.** Townsley & Buckmiller's research primer is free and is the right entry point, and its own framing is the key honest note: the argument for SBG leans heavily on the observation that **traditional percentage grading has been used for a century with essentially no supporting research** — i.e., the case is largely comparative-by-default rather than positive-evidence. The empirical results on SBG are **mixed**, and much of the literature is qualitative and small. SBG's real, checkable contributions are (a) separating academic achievement from behavioral factors (compliance, participation, timeliness) so that a grade means one thing, and (b) reporting against standards rather than an accumulation of points. Whether SBG raises achievement is **not established**. Contested — flag it.

**Retakes/reassessment.** Findings are genuinely mixed on achievement (some improvement attributable to practice effects and test familiarity rather than learning; some studies null), but **consistent** on lowered test anxiety and higher student satisfaction. The design detail that matters: **targeted retakes on specific standards, gated behind a reflection and remediation requirement**, are what practitioners report as workable — unlimited ungated retakes are the version that invites gaming.

**The counter-evidence that must be in the chapter.** Fordham's 2025 nationally representative RAND American Teacher Panel survey (n=967, fall 2024) found teachers largely rejecting the reform package: **81% say no-zeros policies harm engagement**, 56% say the same of no-late-penalties, split on unlimited retakes, 59% think participation grades help engagement, 71% prioritize "high expectations for everyone." Report it precisely for what it is — **teacher perceptions, with no student-outcome data in the report** — and note that it comes from an outlet with a stated position. Both the reform and the counter-reform literatures here are heavier on advocacy than on causal identification.

**Grade inflation is the ambient fact.** ACT-tested GPA rose from about **3.17 (2010) to 3.36 (2021)**, and from 3.22 to 3.39 between 2016 and 2021 while **average ACT fell 20.8 → 20.3**; the 2018-2021 stretch inflated faster than the preceding eight years. The 2019 NAEP High School Transcript Study found students taking more demanding coursework with higher GPAs alongside declining 12th-grade NAEP reading and math. By 2022, **89%+ of high schoolers received an A or B in math, English, social studies and science**. This is the honest complication for grading reform: whatever its equity merits, it is being introduced into a system where the signal in grades is already degrading, and reforms that raise grades without raising learning are indistinguishable from further inflation at the transcript level.

**Homework, by grade band.** Cooper, Robinson & Patall (2006), 60 studies 1987-2003: effect on achievement is real, replicated, and **strongly moderated by grade level** — roughly **d≈0.50 in high school, d≈0.25 in middle school, statistically indistinguishable from zero in elementary**. Correlational versions: r≈0 primary, 0.07 middle, 0.25 high school. Largest effects for 12th graders at 7-12 hours/week, with the authors' own caution that no hard rules are warranted. The "**10-minute rule**" (10 minutes × grade level, from grade 1) is Cooper & Valentine (2001) and is a practical heuristic, **not** an effect-size finding — say so. The administrator translation: an elementary homework policy is a family-relations and habit-formation decision, not an achievement decision; a high school one is both.

### Chapter 8 — Testing burden, opt-out, and accommodations

**Burden, measured.** The Council of the Great City Schools 2015 inventory (free on ERIC, 164 pp, 66 districts): **401 unique tests**; the average student faces about **112 tests between pre-K and grade 12**, roughly **8 per year**; testing consumes about **2.3-2.34% of school time**, or roughly **20-25 hours a year**, with the 8th grade figure at 2.3%. CGCS's own conclusion is more useful than the raw hours: many of the tests were **redundant, misaligned to standards, and not informative about mastery**. So the defensible framing is that the burden problem is primarily one of **duplication and low utility**, not of raw minutes — a district can cut testing time substantially by auditing overlap without touching state requirements.

**Opt-out.** New York is the epicenter and the numbers in circulation conflict badly: some reporting claimed 50% statewide with districts at 70-80%, while more careful figures put New York at about **20% (2015) and 21% (2016)**. Report the range and the discrepancy rather than picking one. Motivations documented: opposition to CCSS and its aligned tests, cumulative test/test-prep volume, and teacher-evaluation stakes attached to scores. **ESSA (2015) retained the 95% participation requirement** and requires states to factor low participation into school accountability ratings — but leaves the *how* to states, so consequences vary state to state. USED told states even under ESSA they need a plan for high opt-out rates. Note also that ESSA does not create a federal right to opt out; the right, where it exists, is a state or district matter.

**Accommodations.** NCEO (University of Minnesota, free) is the trove: an Accommodations Toolkit, a Special Education Directors Toolkit, an ELs-with-Disabilities toolkit, and searchable research summaries with bibliographies on accommodations and alternate assessments. The substantive principle to carry: an accommodation is valid only when it removes a construct-irrelevant barrier — read-aloud on a **reading-decoding** measure changes the construct, read-aloud on a **math** measure usually does not — and the same accommodation can be legitimate on one test and invalidating on another. Also carry the instructional-continuity rule: assessment accommodations should mirror documented instructional accommodations, not appear for the first time on test day. Legal requirements route to `education-law`; technical validity of accommodated scores routes to `testing-and-credentialing-policy`.

## 4. Use-cases for a K-12 administrator

Enumerated distinctly, not blurred:

1. **Run a curriculum adoption.** Sequence: define non-negotiables from your standards → screen with EdReports gateways and your state's tiered reviews → check efficacy separately on WWC/Evidence for ESSA → pilot → budget PD at multiples of the materials cost. The evidence says the PD budget line, not the selection decision, is where the effect lives.
2. **Defend an adoption decision to a board or a community.** You need the honest version — Chingos/Whitehurst plus Louisiana on one side, Kane/Blazar's null plus the Fordham critique on the other — because someone will find the null study.
3. **Audit standards alignment after a state standards revision or rebrand.** Determine what actually changed in the standards versus what only changed name, then decide whether materials, pacing, or only crosswalk documents need work.
4. **Diagnose an intended-vs-implemented gap.** Use the TIMSS vocabulary and the TNTP method: pull a sample of actual student assignments and ask whether they are grade-level, rather than asking whether teachers are on pace.
5. **Cut testing burden without violating state requirements.** Run a CGCS-style inventory of every assessment given, map each to its purpose and its consumer, and eliminate duplicates and tests nobody acts on. This is the single highest-yield audit available in this sub-area.
6. **Decide whether to buy or keep an interim assessment system.** Know that the causal evidence is weak and non-replicating, know that WWC rates the whole data-use apparatus Minimal Evidence, and make the decision on operational grounds (early warning, scheduling, staff time) with honest expectations rather than on an efficacy promise.
7. **Design or reform a grading policy.** Separate the four decisions people conflate: what counts (achievement vs. behavior), what scale (100-point vs. levels), how it aggregates (mean vs. most-recent/decaying), and what recovery exists (retakes, gating, late policy). Bring the evidence and the teacher-perception counter-evidence to the table together — the Fordham survey predicts your implementation resistance precisely.
8. **Set a homework policy by grade band.** Elementary: no achievement justification, decide on other grounds. Middle: modest. High school: real. Use the 10-minute rule as a communication device, labeled as a heuristic.
9. **Build a defensible accommodations process.** Ensure test accommodations mirror instructional accommodations, and screen every accommodation for construct-relevance on that specific assessment.
10. **Prepare for opt-out.** Know your state's ESSA participation-rate consequence rules, your board's authority, and what you may and may not say to families — and have a plan before the rate spikes, since ESSA leaves the accountability treatment to the state.
11. **Vet an "evidence-based" vendor claim.** Ask which ESSA tier, from which study, with what n, how many sites, and whether the study's setting and sample overlap yours — the WWC overlap rule is the question vendors are least prepared for.
12. **Design curriculum-based professional learning** rather than generic PD — the condition under which curriculum effects appear at all.
13. **Communicate achievement to families** in an environment of documented grade inflation, where an A no longer separates and a standards-based report card is trying to restore a signal that percentage grades lost.

## 5. Cross-links

**`college-course-design-and-pedagogy` — already covered; do NOT repeat.** Backward design (Wiggins & McTighe), constructive alignment (Biggs), Bloom's revised taxonomy, syllabus construction, depth-vs-coverage. Write only the **K-12 delta**, which is substantial and is itself a chapter-worthy seam:

- In college the instructor *is* the curriculum author; in K-12 the curriculum is **adopted at district or state level** and the teacher is an implementer. Backward design in K-12 therefore mostly happens **upstream of the classroom**, inside publishers and adoption committees.
- K-12 has **externally mandated standards** with legal and accountability force; college has disciplinary norms and, at most, program accreditation. Alignment is an audit object in K-12, a professional courtesy in college.
- K-12 has **vertical articulation** across 13 grades — coherence and learning progressions across years, which has no real college analogue.
- K-12 grades carry **compulsory-attendance and equity-law** freight, and its grading reform debate (zeros, retakes, minimum grading) has no college counterpart at the same intensity.
- **HQIM, EdReports, state adoption cycles, pacing guides, opt-out** — all K-12-only. None of this exists in the college primer.

**`learning-science-foundations`** — owns the theory. Cite it, don't restate it: retrieval practice and spacing are the mechanism behind why frequent low-stakes checks work; cognitive load explains why "mile wide inch deep" coverage fails; feedback timing/specificity theory underlies formative assessment. This primer supplies the **institutional implementation and the disappointing field effect sizes** where the lab effects shrink.

**`testing-and-credentialing-policy`** — owns psychometrics, validity theory, reliability, standard setting, large-scale accountability testing, and score-use policy. This primer stops at the **classroom/district boundary** and hands over: score interpretation and validity of accommodated scores, state test technical quality, and accountability design. The one deliberate overlap to keep is the **ESSA 95% participation rule**, because opt-out is a school-management problem here and a policy problem there.

**`education-law`** — owns IDEA/504 accommodation entitlements, ESSA statutory requirements, FERPA constraints on data systems and data teams, and any legal exposure from grading and promotion policy. This primer states the *practice* rule (accommodations must mirror instruction; must be construct-relevant) and routes entitlement questions there.

**Suggested new adjacency, if the codex doesn't have it:** a `teacher-professional-learning` seam. Curriculum-based professional learning (Short & Hirsh 2020) is the pivot on which the entire curriculum-effects literature turns, and it currently has no obvious home — it is neither materials nor assessment.
