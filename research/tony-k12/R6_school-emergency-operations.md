## 2. Findings — candidate chapters

### Chapter 1 — The Emergency Operations Plan and the federal six-step process: the spine every other chapter hangs on

The organizing artifact in American K-12 school safety is the **Emergency Operations Plan (EOP)**, and the organizing *method* is the six-step planning process in the 2013 federal *Guide for Developing High-Quality School EOPs* — issued jointly by the U.S. Departments of Education, Homeland Security, Justice (FBI), Health and Human Services, and FEMA, and explicitly written to replace the 2007 *Practical Information on Crisis Planning*. This is the document a principal's whole safety program should trace back to, and the single most important thing to teach is that it reframed school safety from a binder of scenarios into a **process**.

The six steps, verbatim from the guide:

1. **Form a Collaborative Planning Team.** Outcome: a team with representatives from all necessary stakeholders — critically, this is *not* an administrator writing a plan alone. It expects first responders (law enforcement, fire, EMS), public and mental health partners, and — "to the maximum extent appropriate" — students and parents including those with disabilities and access/functional needs.
2. **Understand the Situation.** Identify threats and hazards and assess the risk and vulnerabilities they pose. Output is a **prioritized (high/medium/low) list**. This is the step that contains the assessment sub-disciplines: **site assessments, culture/climate assessments, school behavioral threat assessments, and capacity assessments.** (Note the guide names threat assessment as a *planning input*, not just an incident response — Chapter 4.)
3. **Determine Goals and Objectives.** Decide which prioritized threats/hazards the plan will address (the guide recommends going beyond just "high"). For each, write **at least three goals — before, during, after** — with objectives under each. This before/during/after triad is the guide's practical stand-in for the five mission areas.
4. **Plan Development (Identifying Courses of Action).** Develop the actual courses of action — the what/who/when/where/why/how — and the response protocols and procedures. Scenarios are built *here*, in service of testing courses of action, rather than being the top-level organizing unit.
5. **Plan Preparation, Review, and Approval.** Write it, review it, get it formally approved.
6. **Plan Implementation and Maintenance.** Train, exercise (drills), evaluate, and revise. A plan that isn't trained and exercised is, in the guide's own logic, not implemented.

Under this sits **PPD-8's five preparedness mission areas**, which the guide adopts verbatim and which every state and vendor program now echoes:

- **Prevention** — capabilities to avoid, deter, or stop an imminent crime or threatened/actual mass-casualty incident.
- **Protection** — ongoing capabilities to secure the school and protect people, networks, and property from a threat or hazard.
- **Mitigation** — capabilities to eliminate or reduce loss of life and property by lessening an event's impact, *and* to reduce the likelihood that threats/hazards happen at all.
- **Response** — capabilities to stabilize an emergency once it has happened or is certain to, establish a safe/secure environment, save lives and property, and transition to recovery.
- **Recovery** — capabilities to restore the *learning environment* after an event.

Two framings worth carrying into the primer. First, the guide maps these five onto **before / during / after** to spare educators the emergency-management jargon — Prevention/Protection/Mitigation are mostly "before," Response is "during," Recovery spans "during and after." Second, and this is the honest evidentiary note for the whole chapter: **this is federal guidance, not a mandate and not an efficacy claim.** The guide states plainly that it "does not create any requirements beyond those included in applicable law" and constitutes "informal guidance." Its authority is expert-consensus and inter-agency endorsement, not a randomized trial that plans built this way save more lives. That is true of most of the EOP architecture and should be stated rather than dressed up.

**Plan structure.** A high-quality EOP in this model has a **basic plan** (purpose, situation, concept of operations, org/assignment of responsibilities), **functional annexes** (cross-cutting functions that apply to many hazards — communications, evacuation, lockdown, shelter-in-place, accounting for all persons, family reunification, continuity, recovery, public health/medical, security), and **threat/hazard-specific annexes** (the specific-hazards of Chapter 8). The functional-annex-first design is deliberately the same insight the SRP encodes at the classroom level (Chapter 2): build around *functions/actions*, not an ever-growing list of scenarios.

### Chapter 2 — Standardized response: the Standard Response Protocol (Hold / Secure / Lockdown / Evacuate / Shelter)

The dominant classroom-and-building action language in U.S. and Canadian schools is the **Standard Response Protocol (SRP)** from the **"I Love U Guys" Foundation** — founded in 2006 by John-Michael and Ellen Keyes after the Platte Canyon (Colorado) school shooting that killed their daughter Emily; her final text gave the foundation its name. The SRP's core design claim, and the reason it spread, is that it is **action-based, not scenario-based**: rather than a separate protocol for fire vs. weather vs. intruder, it defines a small set of directives that map onto *any* situation. Five actions, each with a fixed public directive:

- **Hold** — "In Your Room or Area." Used to keep hallways clear (e.g., a medical event or fight in a corridor). Class continues; nobody enters or leaves the hall.
- **Secure** — "Get Inside. Lock Outside Doors." The perimeter action for an external threat (police activity nearby, a dangerous animal). Business continues *inside* a locked building.
- **Lockdown** — "Locks, Lights, Out of Sight." The internal-threat action: lock the individual room, kill lights, move out of sight, silence, do not open the door.
- **Evacuate** — announced with a destination ("Evacuate to the bus lot"). Moving people from one place to another.
- **Shelter** — announced with the **hazard and the safety strategy** ("Shelter for tornado — evac-to-shelter" or "Shelter for hazmat — seal the room"). The action for environmental hazards.

The value for an administrator is **common vocabulary across a district and with responders** — a substitute teacher, a bused-in athlete, and a responding officer all mean the same thing by "Secure." Adoption is very large (the foundation and multiple state safety centers cite 50,000+ schools; the foundation's own SRP page does not publish a hard number, so cite the range, not a precise figure). The foundation distributes the materials free and asks adopters to sign an MOU. It is endorsed/hosted by state bodies including the Texas School Safety Center and adopted by state education departments (e.g., New York State ED).

**Terminology precision the primer must protect** (this is exactly the confusion NASP/NASRO flag in Chapter 6): "Lockdown" specifically means the *locked-room, hide* action for a threat inside; "Secure" (sometimes called "secure the perimeter" or, in older language, "lockout") means *carry on inside a locked building* for a threat outside. Conflating them — announcing "lockdown" when you mean "secure" — is a real operational error that traumatizes unnecessarily and erodes trust in the system. Note also that SRP is one of several standardized systems; some districts use their own color/number codes or the "Run-Hide-Fight" family (Chapter 5). Evidence grade: SRP's benefit is **operational/consensus** (shared language, responder interoperability), not an efficacy RCT showing lives saved vs. an alternative protocol. Say so.

### Chapter 3 — Standardized reunification: the Standard Reunification Method

The least-drilled and most-underestimated function in a real school emergency is **getting students back to their families without losing accountability.** The companion to SRP is the **Standard Reunification Method (SRM)**, also from "I Love U Guys." Its premise: after an evacuation or a shooting, hundreds of frightened parents converge, and an ad-hoc "come get your kid" collapses chain of custody — the school can no longer prove which adult took which child, or account for who is still missing, injured, or worse. SRM imposes structure so the **school retains custody accountability throughout.**

The SRM's six steps: **Greeting → Parents Fill Out a Reunification Card → Checkers Verify ID → Reunification Area → Student Reunification → Accountability.** The engine is the **perforated Reunification Card** — one student per card (recommended), perforated so the top and bottom carry matching information; the parent fills it, the card is separated, and the physical handoff of a card half both documents custody and gives the parent tangible feedback of progress. Off-site reunification (at a pre-arranged partner site away from the incident) is the expected case, not the exception, because the school itself is often an active scene.

Administrator-relevant points: reunification is a **planning function with named roles** (greeters, checkers, runners, reunifiers, an accountability lead), not something to improvise; it must be **exercised** like any drill; and it interlocks with the release/custody law the school already lives under (who is authorized to take a child — the same records that govern normal pickup). Evidence grade: **consensus best practice**, widely adopted alongside SRP; its justification is accountability logic and after-action lessons, not experimental evidence. The most common failure mode is a district that adopts SRP for response but never builds or drills the reunification side — the half that families experience most directly.

### Chapter 4 — Behavioral threat assessment: the strongest-evidenced prevention layer, in two flavors

If any part of this domain approaches an evidence base worth the name, it is **behavioral threat assessment and management (BTAM)** — the practice of identifying students who are on a pathway to violence and intervening *before* an attack, rather than predicting "dangerousness." The federal survey basis: NCES (2024) found **85% of U.S. schools reported using threat-assessment teams**, and multiple states now mandate them (notably Florida after Parkland, 2018). Two authorities dominate, and they are complementary rather than competing.

**(a) The Secret Service NTAC model.** NTAC's work runs from the *Safe School Initiative* (2002) and *Bystander Study* (2008) through *Protecting America's Schools* (2019, 41 completed attacks 2008-2017) to *Averting Targeted School Violence* (2021, 67 disrupted plots by 100 plotters, 2006-2018). Its 2018 operational guide, *Enhancing School Safety Using a Threat Assessment Model*, lays out **eight steps** to build a program:

1. Establish a **multidisciplinary team** (admin, faculty, staff, counselor/psychologist, and available SRO) that directs and *documents* the process.
2. **Define behaviors** — the prohibited ones that trigger immediate intervention (threats, weapons, violence) and the concerning ones that trigger a threat assessment.
3. **Establish a central reporting mechanism** — online form, app, email, phone — that allows **anonymity** and is actually monitored and followed up.
4. **Determine the threshold for law-enforcement intervention**, especially where there is a safety risk.
5. **Establish assessment procedures** built around eleven investigative themes: **motive, communications, inappropriate interests, weapons access, stressors, emotional/developmental issues, desperation/despair, violence as an option, concerned others, capacity, planning, consistency, and protective factors.**
6. **Develop risk-management options** — individualized management plans, not one-size punishment.
7. **Create a safe school climate** of trust and connectedness so students *will* report.
8. **Train all stakeholders** — staff, students, parents, law enforcement.

The empirical spine under this from the Averting study is specific and quotable: plotters were **most often motivated by a grievance with classmates**; the people who noticed the warning communications were **overwhelmingly the plotter's own friends, classmates, and peers** (hence the bystander-reporting emphasis, and the finding that many who noticed did *not* report); **families reported eight of the plots**; plots clustered on **symbolic dates, especially in April** (Columbine's April 20 anniversary); and most plotters intending to use firearms had **unimpeded access, usually from the home.** Crucial honesty flag: NTAC states outright that this is **case-study research** on 67 plots (with background detail on only 43 of 100 plotters) — "not designed for establishing generalizable truths or making predictions." It is a source of *warning behaviors and program design*, not a predictive instrument.

**(b) The Comprehensive School Threat Assessment Guidelines (CSTAG / Cornell).** Developed by Dewey Cornell and colleagues at the University of Virginia (originally VSTAG, from 2001), CSTAG is the operational manual most school teams actually run, and it is **the only school-threat-assessment model supported by controlled studies** (per an independent review, Jackson & Viljoen 2024). Its central move is the **transient vs. substantive distinction**: most student threats are **transient** — jokes, rhetoric, anger, "I'll kill you" as bluster — with no genuine intent, and *require no protective action*; a minority are **substantive** — reflecting real intent, shown by planning, recruitment of accomplices, weapon acquisition. The point of the distinction is to **stop overreacting to normal youthful misbehavior while concentrating resources on the rare serious case.** The **five-step decision tree** (a more parsimonious restatement of the original seven):

1. **Evaluate the threat** — interview the student, target, and witnesses; record exact content and circumstances.
2. **Attempt to resolve as transient** — if it's humor/anger the student retracts or explains with no future intent, it's transient; assessment ends, no security action.
3. **Respond to a substantive threat** — protect potential victims, warn the target and their parents, notify the threatener's parents, look to resolve the conflict, discipline where appropriate. (Sub-classified "serious substantive," e.g., a fight, vs. "very serious substantive," e.g., a threat to kill or use a weapon.)
4. **Conduct a safety evaluation for very serious substantive threats** — a mental-health-led safety-planning interview producing a safety plan.
5. **Implement and monitor the safety plan** — document, maintain contact, revise.

**The evidence chain (the reason CSTAG earns the strongest grade in this file):**
- **Six controlled studies** compare CSTAG schools to controls. The first (Cornell et al., 2009; 95 CSTAG vs. 131 local-procedure vs. 54 no-model high schools) found less bullying, more help-seeking, more positive views of staff, and **one-third fewer long-term suspensions** in CSTAG schools after controls.
- A **randomized controlled trial** (Cornell, Allen & Fan, 2012; 40 schools, half randomized to training, 20 delayed as controls): across ~201 identified threats in a year, CSTAG students were **~4× more likely to get counseling, ~2.5× more likely to get a parent conference, about one-third as likely to receive long-term suspension, and one-eighth as likely to be transferred.** The honest caveat the authors themselves flag: **fidelity varied widely, and schools that followed the model more closely got better results** — implementation, not the model on a shelf, is the active ingredient.
- Classification reliability (Burnette et al., 2018; 844 cases, 339 schools): inter-rater agreement on transient/substantive was **70% (κ=.53)** — real but imperfect; substantive threats were **36× more likely to be attempted** than transient ones (though only 2.5% of all threats were attempted).
- **Statewide implementation** (Florida, post-Parkland mandate; Kerere et al. 2024/2025): in **14,126 cases across 1,692 schools**, ~10.9% resulted in an attack (mostly minor fights), **0.4% a serious injury, none by firearm, none fatal**; ~one-third of substantive vs. under one-tenth of transient threats led to an attempt — the distinction holds at scale. Referral base rate ~**1.5% of enrollment/year** (highest in middle schools), useful for staffing.
- **Equity** — the strongest counter to the civil-rights objection that threat assessment will fall hardest on Black students and students with disabilities: multiple large Virginia and Florida studies found **little or no racial/ethnic or disability disparity in threat-assessment dispositions**, and marked reductions in exclusionary discipline overall. This is contested by some civil-rights groups; report both the objection and the data.
- **Recognition:** in 2013 the model (as VSTAG) became **the only threat-assessment program listed as an evidence-based practice on the federal NREPP registry** — with the asterisk that the executive branch *closed* the NREPP website in 2018, so the registry is defunct and can't be pointed to as a live badge.

The clean bottom line for the primer: **threat assessment is the prevention practice with the most credible evidence in this whole domain — and the evidence is largely about diverting students to support and away from suspension/arrest, not a proven reduction in shootings** (attacks are too rare to power that test). Fidelity of implementation is the variable that matters most. NTAC gives you the program-build blueprint and the warning-behavior research; CSTAG gives you the case-level decision procedure and the controlled evidence. A well-run school uses both.

### Chapter 5 — Response philosophy: traditional lockdown vs. options-based (ALICE / Run-Hide-Fight)

The sharpest live debate in school response is **single-option lockdown vs. multi-option ("options-based") response.** The traditional lockdown — lock, lights off, hide, silent, wait — is near-universal (95% of public schools drilled lockdown as of 2015-16) and is the SRP "Lockdown" action. The critique, pressed hardest by **ALICE** (Alert, Lockdown, Inform, Counter, Evacuate) and echoed by the DHS-origin **Run-Hide-Fight**, is that a single passive option is wrong when a threat is already at or moving toward your room: people should be empowered to **evacuate when they can and, as a last resort, disrupt/counter the attacker.** The mappings line up cleanly — Run≈Evacuate, Hide≈Lockdown, Fight≈Counter — with ALICE adding Alert/Inform (real-time information sharing) that Run-Hide-Fight doesn't emphasize.

Honest state of the evidence — and this is where the primer must not take a vendor's word:
- The claims that "options-based is more effective than lockdown" and that "ALICE teachers feel empowered while lockdown teachers feel anxious" come **largely from research associated with the approach's proponents and from perception/feeling measures**, not from independent trials measuring casualties. There is **no rigorous, independent causal evidence** establishing that any one response philosophy saves more lives in a real attack — the events are too rare and no one can randomize a shooting.
- Traditional lockdown likewise has **little high-quality efficacy evidence**; its dominance is historical and consensus-driven, not proven-superior.
- The **"Counter"/"Fight" component is the most contested piece**, especially for young children; critics (including many school psychologists) argue teaching elementary students to attack an armed adult is developmentally inappropriate and can be traumatizing, while proponents frame it strictly as a last resort for older students/adults.

The defensible framing: the field has **converged on the principle that responders and staff should have more than one option** and that rigid single-option lockdown is a poor universal rule — but **the specific packaged programs are marketed well beyond their evidence**, and the "Counter" element's age-appropriateness is genuinely unsettled. NASP/NASRO's position (Chapter 6) is essentially this: incorporate an options-based mindset *for adults' decision-making*, without turning it into full-scale simulations run on children.

### Chapter 6 — Drills: types, frequency, and the active-shooter-drill controversy (the thinnest evidence, the loudest practice)

This is the chapter where practice most outruns evidence, and a principal most needs a straight answer.

**The landscape.** Fire drills are old, frequent, state-mandated, and uncontroversial — the model of a drill that works. On top of them, states have layered **lockdown / armed-assailant / active-shooter drill mandates**, often without defining terms or specifying method, which NASP and NASRO explicitly blame for confusion between a **lockdown drill** (practicing the locked-hide action — low-intensity, appropriate), an **options-based drill**, and a **full-scale simulation** (sensory realism — actors, "gunfire," fake blood — high-intensity, high-harm). Conflating these is the core operational and ethical error.

**The evidence on active-shooter drills — graded honestly:**
- **Effectiveness:** there is **no strong, conclusive evidence** that active-shooter drills prevent shootings or reduce casualties when one occurs. Student homicides at school are extraordinarily rare (Everytown's framing: on the order of once every several thousand school-years per school). We are drilling intensively for a very-low-base-rate event with no demonstrated protective payoff.
- **Harm:** the **National Academies consensus study** (the fairest single source) finds the research base "critically limited" — **fewer than two dozen empirical studies**, over a third from a single research team in central New York, **only one used random assignment**, and **none studied students with functional or access needs.** Within that thin base, **10-65% of students showed negative emotional reactions**, and students often report feeling *more prepared but less safe.* The **Everytown/Georgia Tech social-media study** (114 schools, 33 states, 90-day pre/post) found **+42% anxiety/stress and +39% depression language, persisting 90+ days**, and reaching parents. The most credible summary is the National Academies' own: **mixed and limited evidence, some preparedness benefit, documented distress for a meaningful minority, and an urgent need for better research** — *not* "drills are proven harmful" and *not* "drills are proven safe."
- **Populations at elevated risk:** neurodivergent students (ADHD, autism), students with prior trauma exposure, and those with IEP/504 plans — precisely the group with **no research coverage.**

**The consensus best-practice line (NASP + NASRO, NCTSN):** you can build preparedness *without* inflicting psychological harm. Concretely: **announce drills in advance**; make them **developmentally appropriate**; **do not run full-scale/sensory simulations as part of students' routine**; use **discussion-based exercises, workshops, and orientations** so students can talk through procedures first; **accommodate IEP/504 students and those with trauma histories**; and if any high-fidelity exercise is run, restrict participants to **screened adult volunteers with parental permission for any student**, excluding anyone with a history of severe traumatic stress. NASP/NASRO also stress **terminology accuracy** (Chapter 2's Secure-vs-Lockdown point). Recent federal attention (a 2024 executive-order push for trauma-informed drill guidelines) reflects the same direction of travel.

The primer's honest verdict: **drilling the *procedure* is defensible and cheap; simulating the *attack* is where evidence is absent and harm is documented.** An administrator can be fully compliant with a state drill mandate using announced, options-informed, discussion-and-walk-through drills, and should treat any pressure toward sensory simulations as the high-risk, low-evidence choice it is.

### Chapter 7 — NIMS/ICS for schools, hazard/vulnerability assessment, mitigation, and continuity of operations

**NIMS/ICS.** The federal guide directs schools to use the **National Incident Management System (NIMS)** and its **Incident Command System (ICS)** when executing response and recovery. The point for schools — which are *not* traditional response organizations but always respond first — is a **standardized, scalable command structure and common terminology** so a school folds cleanly into a **unified command** with arriving police/fire/EMS instead of colliding with it. Practically: pre-assign ICS roles (incident commander — usually the principal until responders arrive — plus operations, planning, logistics, and a safety/PIO function), and train them. FEMA's free IS-100/IS-700-level courses (and school-specific variants) are the standard on-ramp. Evidence grade: consensus/interoperability rationale, not efficacy-tested — but its value (everyone using the same command language and org chart) is concrete and hard to argue with.

**Hazard/vulnerability and site assessment.** This is Step 2 of the process made operational. A school runs **site (physical security) assessments, culture/climate assessments, behavioral-threat-assessment capacity checks, and capacity assessments** to produce the prioritized high/medium/low hazard list. SchoolSafety.gov's **Site Assessment Tool (SSAT)** and the K-12 School Security Guide are the free federal instruments; older ED "Guide to School Vulnerability Assessments" material survives on state DOE sites. The vulnerability concept is specifically the **characteristics of the building/grounds/IT/electrical/surroundings** that make it more susceptible to an identified hazard — and the assessment is strategic (e.g., **long rural responder times change the calculus** and may demand compensating measures).

**Mitigation vs. protection — a distinction schools blur.** Protection is ongoing hardening (locked doors, access control, visitor management); mitigation is **reducing impact and likelihood** — safe-area/"hard corner" design (Marjory Stoneman Douglas Commission), buffering hazardous storage, addressing structural vulnerabilities, and prevention-layer work like climate/bullying programs. SchoolSafety.gov's *Mitigation for Schools* PDF is the concrete list.

**Continuity of Operations (COOP).** The most-skipped annex. After an incident, which functions **must continue or restart fast**? The recurring list: **continuity of instruction, parent/community communication, special-education service delivery (an IDEA obligation that does not pause), meal operations, payroll/finance, records/data preservation, and preservation of evidence.** COOP interlocks with the cyber hazard (Chapter 8) because a ransomware event *is* a continuity event. Evidence grade: pure planning discipline; the value is avoiding the second crisis (services collapse) that follows the first.

### Chapter 8 — Specific hazards and the recovery/psychological-first-aid tail

**Specific-hazard annexes** are the "before/during/after" playbooks for the threats the assessment prioritized. The recurring set for a K-12 school:
- **Severe weather** (tornado/hurricane/severe storm) → the SRP **Shelter** action; pre-identified interior shelter areas; NOAA/warning integration.
- **Fire** → the mature, drilled baseline; evacuation routes and accountability.
- **Medical emergency** → staff first-aid/CPR/AED, stop-the-bleed, anaphylaxis/EpiPen and asthma protocols, and the SRP **Hold** action to keep corridors clear; the most *frequent* real emergency by far.
- **Intruder / armed assailant** → **Lockdown**/options-based response (Chapters 5-6) plus the threat-assessment prevention layer (Chapter 4).
- **Cyber incident** (ransomware, data breach, "swatting"/false-alarm calls) → a genuine and growing hazard for schools; the partner body is **MS-ISAC/CIS** for K-12 cyber-incident response, and the continuity/COOP annex is where it lives. FERPA/data-protection *law* routes to the privacy primer; the *operational* incident response belongs here.
- Plus locale-specific hazards (earthquake, wildfire, flood, hazmat near industrial/rail corridors, bus/transportation incidents).

**Recovery and psychological first aid — the tail everyone underplans.** Recovery in the federal model means restoring the **learning environment**, and it has four sub-domains schools routinely under-resource: **physical/structural** (reopening the building), **business/fiscal** (COOP), **academic** (resuming instruction), and **psychological/emotional** — the hardest and longest. The dominant frameworks:
- **Psychological First Aid (PFA) for Schools** (NCTSN/NCTSN-aligned) — an evidence-*informed* (not RCT-proven) immediate approach: create calm and safety, provide accurate basic information, connect students to support, and identify who needs more.
- **PREPaRE** (NASP) — the school-crisis curriculum that spans the same five mission phases: Workshop 1 (prevention/preparedness — building the systems) and Workshop 2 (intervention/recovery). Its distinctive contribution is **psychological triage** — primary/secondary/tertiary — to identify who is at highest risk for trauma based on **physical and emotional proximity to the event, pre-crisis vulnerabilities, and severity of reactions**, so scarce clinical resources go where they're needed. Recovery also means **memorials done carefully** (avoiding contagion/glamorization, especially after a suicide), phased staff support, and anniversary planning.

Evidence grade across the recovery tail: **evidence-informed, expert-consensus**. PFA is widely endorsed and low-risk but not RCT-validated for outcomes; PREPaRE is the profession's standard curriculum but is a *training program*, not an efficacy-tested intervention. The honest framing is that these give a **structured, humane, do-no-additional-harm** approach — which is exactly the value in the chaotic aftermath — while the deep clinical treatment (trauma/PTSD) routes to the existing trauma and mental-health-clinical primers.

### Chapter 9 — Catholic and private-school applicability (where it differs)

Almost everything above applies to a Catholic or independent school, with real differences a Seton-audience principal must know:

- **Governance and the plan-approval loop.** A public school's EOP is approved through a district and state chain; a Catholic school's is typically owned by the school but **reviewed and collected annually by the (arch)diocesan Office of Catholic Schools**, with a diocesan safety committee and an annually-updated checklist (the Diocese of Corpus Christi model is a clean exemplar — SRP + SRM adopted diocese-wide, each school's plan reviewed annually by the OCS). The diocese functions as the "district" for standardization, but authority and resourcing are thinner.
- **Standardized protocols travel well.** SRP/SRM are explicitly free and non-proprietary and are widely adopted by dioceses precisely because they impose district-like common language without a public district. This is a strong, cheap recommendation for a Catholic school.
- **SROs and funding.** State SRO funding programs are **usually restricted to public schools**; a private/Catholic school generally must **contract** for law-enforcement presence via a valid agreement (MOU) with the local department, and pays for hardening out of its own or diocesan budget — federal/state school-safety **grants are frequently public-school-only**, though some (e.g., certain DHS nonprofit-security and STOP School Violence streams) reach faith-based schools. Verify per-program eligibility; this is a live gap for private schools.
- **Threat assessment still applies, but the referral ecosystem differs** — a diocesan school may lack an in-house psychologist and rely more on community mental-health partners; the CSTAG/NTAC team should be built with that in mind.
- **Statutory mandates vary.** State drill-frequency and threat-assessment-team mandates may or may not bind private schools depending on the state; a Catholic school often adopts them **voluntarily** (or by diocesan policy) even where not legally required. And the safe part to state plainly: **the underlying safety facts, weapon-access realities, and drill-harm evidence do not change with school type** — a private setting is statistically less likely to see an incident (public institutions account for the large majority of school active-shooter events) but is **not exempt** from the planning obligation.

## 4. Use-cases for a K-12 (incl. Catholic/private) administrator

Enumerated distinctly, not blurred:

1. **Build or overhaul the EOP from scratch.** Run the federal six-step process: convene a real multidisciplinary team (Step 1), do the hazard/vulnerability assessment to get a prioritized list (Step 2), set before/during/after goals (Step 3), write functional + hazard annexes (Step 4), get formal approval (Step 5), then train and exercise (Step 6). Use the DHS-mirrored guide as the template.
2. **Standardize response language across the building/district/diocese.** Adopt SRP so a sub, a bused-in athlete, and a responding officer all mean the same thing by "Secure" vs. "Lockdown" — and drill the terminology distinction that is the most common real error.
3. **Stand up a reunification capability, not just a response.** Adopt SRM, assign the roles, pre-arrange an off-site reunification partner, print the cards, and *exercise it* — the half families experience most and schools drill least.
4. **Create and run a behavioral threat-assessment team.** Use NTAC's eight steps to build the program (team, defined behaviors, anonymous central reporting, law-enforcement threshold, procedures, management options, climate, training) and CSTAG's five-step decision tree to run each case — with the transient/substantive distinction as the guardrail against overreaction.
5. **Decide your response philosophy (single-option lockdown vs. options-based) with eyes open.** Know that neither is efficacy-proven, that the field favors *having options for adult decision-making*, and that "Counter"/"Fight" for young children is genuinely contested — don't buy a vendor's certainty.
6. **Design a drill program that satisfies a state mandate without traumatizing students.** Announced, developmentally appropriate, discussion/walk-through based; no sensory full-scale simulations in the student routine; explicit IEP/504 and trauma-history accommodations; volunteers-and-parental-permission for any high-fidelity exercise.
7. **Answer a board/parent/pastor asking "are the active-shooter drills helping or hurting?"** Give the National Academies' honest verdict — mixed, thin evidence; real preparedness perception; documented distress for a meaningful minority; better to drill the procedure than simulate the attack.
8. **Run a hazard/vulnerability and physical-site assessment.** Use SchoolSafety.gov's SSAT and K-12 School Security Guide; account for responder response-time realities; separate mitigation (impact/likelihood reduction, hard corners) from protection (ongoing hardening).
9. **Adopt NIMS/ICS and pre-assign command roles** so the school integrates into unified command with responders instead of colliding — free FEMA IS courses as the on-ramp.
10. **Write the continuity (COOP) annex** for instruction, communication, special-education service delivery, meals, finance, records, and evidence preservation — and treat a cyber/ransomware incident as a continuity event, with MS-ISAC as the partner.
11. **Plan the recovery/psychological tail before you need it.** PFA for immediate response, PREPaRE psychological triage to route scarce clinical help by proximity/vulnerability/reaction severity, careful memorial/anniversary planning — with deep clinical care routed to the trauma/mental-health primers.
12. **Adapt all of the above to a Catholic/private context.** Run the plan through the diocesan Office of Catholic Schools' annual review, adopt the free SRP/SRM to get district-like standardization without a district, contract (via MOU) rather than assume state-funded SROs, and check each grant's public-vs-private eligibility.
