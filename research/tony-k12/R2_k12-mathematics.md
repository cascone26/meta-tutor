## 2. Findings — candidate chapters

### Chapter 1 — The math wars: what the fight is actually about

The "math wars" is the decades-long US controversy between **reform / inquiry / constructivist** and **traditional / explicit / direct** approaches to teaching mathematics. The proximate trigger was NCTM's **1989 *Curriculum and Evaluation Standards***, which elevated problem-solving, real-world context, conceptual reasoning, and student discourse over rote memorization and drill. **California** amplified it with its 1992 framework and then reversed in **1997** when the State Board rejected reform-oriented standards — the flashpoint. The parent-and-mathematician group **Mathematically Correct** organized against reform curricula. Schoenfeld's (2004) "The Math Wars" is the canonical scholarly history.

The two camps, stated neutrally: **traditionalists** hold that reform curricula are superficial and erode classical rigor (fluency with algorithms, proof); **reformers** hold that traditional teaching produces shallow, disconnected procedural knowledge and that proficiency centers reasoning, multiple strategies, and disposition. The dispute is genuinely **values-laden as well as empirical** — it is partly a disagreement about *what mathematical proficiency is*, not only about which method teaches it best. A deeper historical root the explicit camp cites heavily is **Project Follow Through** (1967-1995), the largest US education experiment, in which Direct Instruction produced the strongest gains — including on the higher-order and affective measures inquiry models were expected to win. Report it as **evidence-cited-by-one-side and contested in interpretation** (reform scholars dispute the design, fidelity measurement, and generalizability), not as settled consensus.

The administrator-relevant point: a principal will meet this war as a live faculty and community fault line — over standard algorithms, "invented strategies," timed fact tests, calculators, tracking, and the very look of a math lesson. Knowing that the research has largely **moved past the either/or** (next chapter) is the most useful thing a leader can bring to it.

### Chapter 2 — The convergence: what the current evidence actually shows

The honest state of the field is **not** "one side won." It is a **split by learning phase and learner**, and the integrating principle is the **expertise-reversal effect**: heavy guidance helps novices, and recedes (can even hurt) as prior knowledge grows.

- **Explicit / systematic instruction has the strongest, most consistent evidence for (a) initial acquisition of new content and (b) students who struggle or have a math learning disability.** This is the WWC and NMAP consensus (Chapters 3, 10).
- **The reform tradition owns the higher-order goals** — problem-solving disposition, reasoning and argumentation, the Standards for Mathematical Practice — which explicit-instruction research does not claim to deliver and does not primarily measure.
- **Guided/scaffolded discovery beats both pure discovery and pure telling.**

The single best citation for the nuance is **Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich & Tenenbaum (2011)**, a meta-analysis of 164 studies: **unassisted/pure discovery loses to explicit instruction, d = −0.38** (favoring explicit), while **enhanced discovery — scaffolded, with feedback, worked examples, or elicited explanation — beats other instruction, d = +0.30**; within enhanced discovery, **guided discovery d = 0.50** and **elicited explanation d = 0.36**, while pure "generation" is null. **Evidence grade: strong (large meta-analysis).**

The most authoritative US synthesis, **NMAP's *Foundations for Success* (2008)**, reached the same centerline: for low-achievers and students with math LD, explicit instruction should be "available on a regular basis" with consistently positive effects on computation and word problems — but the panel **explicitly refused** to endorse instruction that is *entirely* student-centered or *entirely* teacher-directed, calling all-encompassing versions of either "not supported by high-quality research." **Evidence grade: strong (expert-panel synthesis).** That refusal is the neutral anchor for a school leader.

### Chapter 3 — Explicit instruction and worked examples for math

**WWC Practice Guide #26 (March 2021), *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics: Intervention in the Elementary Grades*** is the cleanest evidence signal in this whole area: six recommendations, **all six rated Strong** —
1. **Systematic instruction** to develop understanding of mathematical ideas.
2. **Clear, concise mathematical language** and support for students' use of it.
3. A well-chosen set of **concrete and semi-concrete representations** (the CRA progression).
4. **Number lines** to build concepts and procedures (elevated to its own recommendation in 2021).
5. Deliberate **word-problem instruction** attentive to problem structure (schema-based).
6. **Timed activities**, regularly, as one way to build fluency.

This supersedes **WWC PG#2 (2009)**, which spanned elementary *and* middle school with eight recommendations at mixed evidence — only "explicit and systematic instruction" (Strong) and word-problem structure (Strong) reached the top there, while screening (Moderate), visual representations (Moderate), 10-minutes-of-fact-fluency (Moderate), progress monitoring (Minimal), and motivation (Minimal) were lower. The 2021 upgrade to all-Strong reflects a maturing elementary-intervention research base. Two things a leader should hold: PG#2 remains the reference for the **middle grades and for screening/progress-monitoring**, which PG#26 dropped; and **the federal guide affirmatively recommends timed fluency activities** — directly relevant to the anxiety debate in Chapter 6.

The **worked-example effect** (studying fully-worked solutions beats solving equivalent problems, for novices) is cognitive load theory's most-tested prediction, and it is math-strong: **Barbieri et al. (2023)** report an average effect on mathematics performance of **g = 0.48** (55 studies, 181 effects); older/general reviews report larger effects (d ≈ 0.62 secondary, ≈ 0.73 early university), the difference reflecting the newer study's tighter math-only sample. The refinements matter operationally: **example-problem pairs** and **faded worked examples** (progressively removing steps) beat blocks of worked examples, and the effect **reverses with expertise**. **Evidence grade: strong for novices; conditional (weakens/reverses for higher-knowledge learners).** **Rosenshine's (2012) *Principles of Instruction*** is the go-to operational articulation of explicit teaching that math intervention leans on (small steps, models/worked examples, check for understanding, ~80% success before independent practice, scaffold then overlearn) — a synthesis of strong components rather than an RCT itself.

### Chapter 4 — Conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, and the "false dichotomy"

The organizing framework is the National Research Council's ***Adding It Up* (2001)** and its **five intertwined strands of mathematical proficiency** — the braided-rope metaphor, all five developing together (full text free at nationalacademies.org):
1. **Conceptual understanding** — comprehension of concepts, operations, relations.
2. **Procedural fluency** — skill in carrying out procedures **flexibly, accurately, efficiently, and appropriately** (note: explicitly *not* just speed or memorization).
3. **Strategic competence** — formulate, represent, and solve problems.
4. **Adaptive reasoning** — logical thought, reflection, explanation, justification.
5. **Productive disposition** — seeing mathematics as sensible, useful, worthwhile, coupled with belief in one's own efficacy.

The "**fluency OR understanding**" framing is rejected across current scholarship. Baroody (2006) argues fluency is far more than isolated memorized facts; **NCTM's Procedural Fluency position** holds that conceptual understanding is the necessary foundation *on which* fluency is built ("build procedural fluency **from** conceptual understanding"); and **Rittle-Johnson, Schneider & Star (2015), "Not a One-Way Street,"** supplies the strongest empirical statement — the relationship is **bidirectional and iterative**, concepts supporting procedures and practicing procedures deepening concepts, each reinforcing the other over time. **Evidence grade: moderate-to-strong** (the iterative model rests on multiple controlled studies).

The neutrality note a leader must keep: the *theoretical* dichotomy is false, but the *practical* tension is real and unresolved — traditionalists observe that reform implementations often shortchange **automaticity of number facts and standard algorithms**, and NMAP affirms that fact automaticity matters precisely because it frees working memory for problem-solving. So: false as a theory of knowledge, genuine as a fight over classroom emphasis and sequencing. Present both.

### Chapter 5 — Number sense and early numeracy

"Number sense" spans a biologically-primary nonsymbolic layer and the symbolic competencies built on it. The nonsymbolic layer has two mechanisms (Frontiers 2011; Dehaene): **subitizing** — fast, exact apprehension of small sets (≤3-4), precise and effortless — and the **Approximate Number System (ANS)**, an imprecise magnitude estimator on a "mental number line" obeying **Weber's law** (discrimination depends on the *ratio* of quantities). **Established.** Dehaene and Butterworth are the foundational theorists; Butterworth additionally frames a defective number-module as central to dyscalculia (Chapter 11). The **counting principles (Gelman & Gallistel, 1978)** are canonical: one-to-one, stable-order, cardinal, abstraction, and order-irrelevance.

On prediction, be careful and honest. **ANS acuity predicts math but modestly:** the verified preschool study (Mazzocco, Feigenson & Halberda, N=174) found the Weber fraction predicting TEMA-3 scores with **R² ≈ 0.07**, significant after controlling age and vocabulary but **not** full IQ/working memory; across the literature ANS→math correlations run a modest r ≈ 0.2-0.3, and the **causal direction is genuinely unsettled** (does ANS scaffold symbolic math, or does symbolic learning sharpen the ANS?). The **stronger** predictor is **symbolic** early numeracy — magnitude comparison of digits ("which is bigger") and Jordan's **three-strand model** (number knowledge, number *relations*, number operations), where number relations predict across all achievement levels. Jordan's **Number Sense Brief screener** uniquely predicts broad math achievement through elementary school after accounting for reading, age, and general cognition; K and Grade-1 scores predict Grade-3 proficiency. **Evidence grade: strong and replicated for symbolic early-numeracy screening; real-but-small-and-contested for nonsymbolic ANS.** (The screener's exact sensitivity/specificity values need the primary PDF, which did not parse — flagged.)

### Chapter 6 — Fact fluency, automaticity, and the timed-test / anxiety debate

**Why automaticity matters** is nearly uncontested *in principle*: automatic fact retrieval frees working-memory capacity for multi-step problem solving; without it, working memory is consumed recalling basics. The dispute is about *how* to build it. **Bay-Williams & Kling's *Math Fact Fluency*** framework is the widely-adopted professional model: fluency (not memorization) is the goal; it develops in **three phases** — counting → **reasoning strategies** (deriving unknowns from knowns, e.g. 5+6 from 5+5) → **mastery** (efficient retrieval, operationally ~3 seconds via recall *or* an efficient strategy); and fluency means **accurate, efficient, and flexible**, not merely fast. For **intervention**, Codding et al. (2011) found drill-plus-modeling and multi-component packages (cover-copy-compare, incremental rehearsal, taped problems) produce the largest effects. **Grade: professional-consensus framework; moderate single-case evidence for the intervention techniques.**

The genuinely **contested** node is whether **timed tests specifically cause math anxiety**:

- **Position A (Boaler, "Fluency Without Fear," YouCubed):** timed testing is a major onset trigger — "for about one-third of students the onset of timed testing is the beginning of math anxiety," seen as young as age 5 and **not** limited to low achievers (notably high achievers and girls); mechanism via Beilock's finding that stress/time-pressure blocks the working memory that holds math facts. Boaler is pro-practice and pro-fluency but insists fluency grow from number sense, not speed drills. NCTM has stated timed tests do not *assess* fluency.
- **Position B (automaticity essential; the anti-timed stance overstated):** cognitive scientists (e.g. Barbara Oakley) and school-psych researchers argue automaticity is foundational and that discouraging fluency/timed practice harms the struggling and LD students who most need efficient retrieval. The strongest **direct** test — **Maki et al. (2024, *J. School Psychology*)**, 113 fourth/fifth-graders under overt vs. covert timing — found **no statistically significant anxiety difference** ("We did not find that timing causes math anxiety"). Nicole McNeil frames the relationship as complex and conditional, cautioning against "the knee-jerk reaction that timed anything is bad."

**Established:** automaticity frees working memory; math anxiety is real and disrupts working memory (Ashcraft & Kirk 2001, Chapter 12); fluency is flexible-accurate-efficient, not raw speed. **Contested:** the causal "timed tests cause anxiety" claim — the 2024 experiment (small-N, self-report, single study, needs replication) found no effect, while Boaler's central source is a practitioner piece, not peer-reviewed. The honest read for a leader: **build automaticity; the method — especially high-stakes timed tests used as assessment — is where reasonable evidence-based disagreement remains.** Note the tension with WWC PG#26 recommendation 6, which endorses timed *activities* (not high-stakes timed *tests*) as one fluency-building route — a distinction worth preserving.

### Chapter 7 — CRA, manipulatives, representations, and concreteness fading

**CRA (Concrete-Representational-Abstract)** operationalizes **Bruner's enactive → iconic → symbolic** modes: manipulate objects, then draw/represent, then work in symbols. Developed for math intervention (Witzel, Miller, Hudson, Mercer), it blends explicit instruction with guided discovery. The strongest evidence is for **students with LD / math disabilities** across basic operations, fractions, and algebra: **Bouck, Satsangi & Park (2018)** applied formal quality-indicator standards and classified CRA an **evidence-based practice for students with LD** (SAGE full text 403; conclusion from the ERIC abstract), and a **2025 meta-analytic review** (Ebner et al., *RASE*) is the newest quantitative synthesis. **Evidence grade: moderate-to-strong for students with LD, but the body leans on single-case designs** (PND/PAND, not Cohen's d), which limits generalization of magnitudes; general-education evidence is thinner and mostly inferred from the manipulatives literature.

On **manipulatives**, the key meta-analysis is **Carbonneau, Marley & Selig (2013)** — 55 studies, N=7,237, K-college, manipulatives vs. abstract-symbol-only: a statistically significant **small-to-moderate** overall effect, with moderate-to-large effects on **retention** and small effects on problem solving, transfer, and justification. (Widely-cited decimal values — overall d ≈ 0.37, retention ≈ 0.46, transfer ≈ 0.11 — appear in the paper but were **not** confirmable in the ERIC abstract, which reports magnitude bands only; flag as needs-primary-verification.) The load-bearing moderator: **high perceptual richness** (flashy, detailed manipulatives) can *reduce* learning and transfer — the empirical basis for the "manipulatives-as-toys" risk, converging with Uttal/DeLoache's **dual-representation problem** (a manipulative must be grasped as an object *and* as a symbol; young children can fixate on the object). **Sowell (1989)** adds that benefits appear only with **long-term use** (a school year or more) and that pictures did not outperform symbols.

The resolution to the concrete-vs-abstract tension is **concreteness fading** (Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone, 2014): don't choose — **start concrete and explicitly, then gradually fade** through an iconic/representational middle to symbolic form; faded sequences promote **transfer** better than concrete-only or abstract-only. Same enactive→iconic→symbolic arc as CRA, framed as a gradual fade rather than three discrete stops. **Evidence grade: emerging-to-moderate** (positive, but the review flags methodological gaps). Practical takeaway for a leader: manipulatives are not automatically beneficial — **teacher framing, explicit symbol-linking, fading, and duration** determine the payoff, and plain manipulatives beat flashy ones.

### Chapter 8 — The NCTM canon, productive struggle, and CGI

NCTM is the reform side's institutional voice, and four artifacts recur in K-12 math leadership:

**Standards for Mathematical Practice (SMPs)** — the eight *process* standards embedded in Common Core (2010), applying at every grade: (1) make sense of problems and persevere; (2) reason abstractly and quantitatively; (3) construct viable arguments and critique reasoning; (4) model with mathematics; (5) use appropriate tools strategically; (6) attend to precision; (7) look for and make use of structure; (8) look for and express regularity in repeated reasoning. They descend directly from *Adding It Up*'s strands.

***Principles to Actions* (2014) — eight effective Mathematics Teaching Practices:** establish math goals to focus learning; implement tasks that promote reasoning and problem solving; use and connect representations; facilitate meaningful discourse; pose purposeful questions; **build procedural fluency from conceptual understanding**; **support productive struggle**; elicit and use evidence of student thinking. These are research-*informed* NCTM syntheses, not each independently WWC-rated — grade the packaging **expert-consensus/emerging**, though individual components (representations, discourse, formative assessment) carry their own evidence.

***Catalyzing Change* (2018)** is the most controversial: it recommends **discontinuing tracking** into "dead-end" pathways, a **common shared pathway** for roughly the first 2½ years of high school, and dropping the race to calculus as the sole goal. This is a **policy/equity position, not an evidence rating**, and the detracking recommendation is **contested** (Chapter 13). Grade: **position statement / contested policy.** Note that post-2014 NCTM explicitly rejects the fluency-vs-understanding dichotomy — a real moderation from the 1989 posture.

**Productive struggle** (Hiebert & Grouws 2007) — students' effort to make sense of something not immediately apparent, explicitly distinguished from frustration/despair — is Teaching Practice #7 and a core reform mechanism. **Evidence grade: emerging/moderate**: theoretically well-motivated and consistent with desirable-difficulties research, but the direct causal RCT base on "productive struggle" as an intervention is thinner than for explicit instruction, and recent practitioner debate pushes back on struggle applied without scaffolding for novices — which loops back to Alfieri/KSC (struggle helps once students have enough to work with, i.e. a guided/enhanced-discovery condition). Its operational vehicle is **Launch-Explore-Summarize** (Stein et al. 2008), the task-first lesson structure — pose a rich task (launch), students work while the teacher monitors and selects strategies (explore), whole-class discussion sequences and connects strategies toward the target math (summarize), governed by Stein's "5 Practices" (anticipate, monitor, select, sequence, connect). **Evidence grade: emerging; highly implementation-dependent** (the summarize phase is where lessons succeed or fail). It is the deliberate inverse of the explicit I-do/we-do/you-do gradual release; both have evidence in their niches.

**Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI)** is the reform side's flagship *evidenced* program — not a curriculum but a **teacher PD** program that builds teachers' knowledge of children's mathematical thinking (problem-type taxonomies: Join/Separate/Part-Part-Whole/Compare; strategy progressions: direct modeling → counting → derived facts). The original Carpenter/Fennema experiments (1989+) showed CGI students gaining in **both problem-solving and computation** — the reform program did not sacrifice skills. But the honest current read is modest: the **2024 five-year RCT** (22 schools) found intent-to-treat effects of **g ≈ 0.03 in primary grades (near-null)** and **g ≈ 0.16 in intermediate grades**, grade level the only significant moderator, and JRME's "Looking Inside the Black Box" shows the effect is **implementation-sensitive**. **Evidence grade: moderate** — genuinely randomized and replicated (strong design), but small-to-modest and fidelity-dependent effects. State that plainly rather than overselling it.

### Chapter 9 — Coherence of standards progressions and learning trajectories

The Common Core math design rests on three shifts — **Focus** (fewer topics, deeper; the direct answer to Schmidt's critique below), **Coherence** (mathematics as interconnected concepts, realized **cross-grade** — each standard extends prior learning — and **within-grade** — supporting topics feed the major work), and **Rigor** (a balanced pursuit of conceptual understanding, procedural skill, and application, *not* "hard problems"). The three lead writers were **William McCallum**, **Jason Zimba**, and **Phil Daro**. **Evidence grade: strong (documentary)** for the principles as stated; whether the principles *as implemented* raise achievement is the separate, weaker question handled in the sibling standards file.

The coherence is made explicit in two free, verified troves. The **Progressions Documents** (18 narrative PDFs, K-HS, University of Arizona / McCallum, Brookhill-funded) describe how each domain develops across grades — Counting & Cardinality (K) → Operations & Algebraic Thinking (K-5) → Number & Operations in Base Ten (K-5), plus Fractions, Ratios & Proportional Relationships, Expressions & Equations, and the HS conceptual categories — "informed both by research on children's cognitive development and by the logical structure of mathematics." They live free and un-gated at Achieve the Core (the U. Arizona archive is a secondary mirror whose drafts may lag). Zimba's **"wiring diagram"** (a dependency graph of every K-8 standard) became the interactive **Coherence Map**, which lets a coach trace a struggling student's gap *backward* to prerequisite standards — but McCallum stresses it maps *logical dependency, not a teaching sequence*.

**Learning trajectories** are the related research construct: a goal + a **developmental progression** of levels of children's thinking + **instructional tasks** matched to each level (term from Simon 1995). **Clements & Sarama** are the strongest empirical strand — their **Building Blocks** curriculum and the free **learningtrajectories.org (LT²)** tool (birth-grade 3), with TRIAD scale-up RCTs among the better causal evidence that trajectory-based instruction raises early-math outcomes. The **CPRE RR-68 report (Daro, Mosher & Corcoran 2011)** is the policy touchstone and is candid about limits. **Evidence grade — deliberately split:** *descriptive/design validity* is **strong** in well-studied domains (counting, early number, geometry/measurement); *causal achievement evidence* is **moderate for early math (Clements/Sarama), thin for middle/high** — the construct is far better established PreK-2, cross-domain coverage is incomplete, and most trajectories are validated as *sequences*, not as proof that teaching to the trajectory beats alternatives at scale. Do not overstate them as settled causal science outside early math.

The comparative backdrop is **Schmidt's "a mile wide and an inch deep"**: using TIMSS, US 8th-grade math texts had only ~25% of topics *new* since 4th grade (≈75% review), where high-performing "A+ countries" invert the ratio — few topics per grade, treated deeply, in a coherent hierarchical progression. This is the explicit intellectual justification the CCSSM writers cited for Focus and Coherence. **Evidence grade: strong (well-replicated descriptive/comparative)** for the topic-proliferation finding; **weaker** for the causal leap "coherent standards → international achievement," which is confounded by teacher prep, tracking, and out-of-school time. Coherence is necessary, not sufficient.

### Chapter 10 — Math MTSS: screening, progress monitoring, and intervention programs

The math intervention layer has its own tools and its own honest evidence picture. **CBM-Math** is the workhorse for screening and progress monitoring — brief, standardized, repeatable probes (early numeracy; computation "M-COMP"; concepts & applications "M-CAP"), auto-graphed with aim lines. The neutral arbiter of tool quality is the federal **NCII Tools Charts** (free), which rate each tool separately for screening (reliability, validity, **classification accuracy**) and progress monitoring (sensitivity to growth, alternate forms, decision rules), with bias analyses — the honest read is the chart, not the vendor. Example caveat: **easyCBM Math** rates *convincing* on reliability in grades 3-8 but **mixed on validity** (unconvincing in grade 3, convincing grades 6-8), a real limit for early-elementary screening. **Intervention Central** offers free CBM-Math probe generators.

On **programs**, the load-bearing distinction for a leader is that an ESSA "Strong" **label reflects study rigor, not effect magnitude**:

|---|---|---|---|
| **Number Rockets** (Fuchs, Gr-1 Tier-2 tutoring) | Federal RCT (NCEE 2012-4007) | **+0.34** on TEMA-3 (students <38th pctile) | Cleanest design, largest effect, but narrow (grade 1, at-risk) |

### Chapter 11 — Dyscalculia and math learning disabilities

**DSM-5** classifies math LD under **Specific Learning Disorder, "with impairment in mathematics"** (number sense, fact fluency, calculation accuracy, math reasoning); "dyscalculia" is the common term. Prevalence of MLD/dyscalculia specifically is **~3-7%** (commonly 3-6%, "about one child per classroom") — comparable to dyslexia. The **core-deficit theories are genuinely contested**: the **domain-specific** view (Butterworth) posits an innate deficit in the sense of *numerosity* / a "number module" (Butterworth-De Smedt: "a core information-processing deficit"); the **domain-general** view attributes MLD to working memory, processing speed, reasoning, or oral-language deficits. Current consensus leans **heterogeneous** — some children show core numerical deficits, others domain-general, many both; no single cause is settled. Comorbidity is high with **dyslexia** (co-occurrence commonly ~40% among affected) and with **ADHD**.

The **under-identification** relative to dyslexia is well documented and matters to a principal: diagnosis rates are far lower despite similar prevalence, driven by (1) reading problems being more visible than math, (2) social tolerance of "being bad at math," and (3) a **teacher-training gap** — surveys report ~76% of educators with *no* dyscalculia training and only ~3% receiving any pre-service, against far more dyslexia training (Loughborough 2026; UK POST brief). Lower research funding compounds it. **Evidence grade: prevalence and under-identification well-established; core-deficit mechanism contested.**

### Chapter 12 — Math anxiety

**Math anxiety** — a feeling of tension and apprehension that interferes with number manipulation and math problem-solving — is real and mechanistically understood. **Ashcraft & Kirk (2001)** is foundational: high math-anxious individuals show **reduced working-memory span**, especially on calculation-loaded tasks, because intrusive worry consumes WM resources (the "affective drop"). **Prevalence** is high but instrument-dependent — figures range from roughly ~17-25% for severe anxiety in student populations up to ~80% reporting high anxiety in some community-college samples; report the range, not a single number. The relationship with achievement is **bidirectional** (Ramirez, Chang, Maloney, Levine & Beilock 2016), mediated by attentional control / working memory — poorer WM makes anxiety more damaging.

The most **actionable** finding for a leader is intergenerational: **Maloney et al. (2015, *Psychological Science*)**, a grade 1-2 field study, found math-anxious parents' children learned less math and grew more math-anxious over the year — **but only when those parents frequently helped with math homework**. When math-anxious parents helped rarely, there was no transmission. The mechanism is the homework interaction, not genes alone — which points directly at parent-facing guidance and homework design. **Interventions** with an evidence base are brief and low-cost: **expressive writing** before high-stakes math (Park, Ramirez & Beilock — offloads worry, frees WM; reduces anxiety, though downstream performance effects are mixed) and **cognitive reappraisal of arousal** (reframing physiological arousal as helpful — promising, with some exam-score gains). **Evidence grade: the phenomenon and WM mechanism are strong; the interventions are promising-but-modest and context-dependent.** The Frontiers "60 Years" review is the best single open-access primer chapter.

### Chapter 13 — Algebra as a gatekeeper, and the tracking/detracking equity fight

Algebra is the canonical academic gate. **Adelman's *Answers in the Tool Box* (1999) / *The Toolbox Revisited* (2006)** established that the **highest math course completed in high school** is the single strongest curricular predictor of **bachelor's-degree completion** — reaching beyond Algebra 2 into trig/precalc/calculus roughly doubles BA-completion odds. **Bob Moses' Algebra Project** (1982) reframed 8th-grade algebra access as **"math literacy as a civil right"** through a community-organizing model rooted in the SNCC tradition. Those two together make algebra placement one of the most equity-loaded decisions a school makes.

The policy record is a warning against both extremes. **"Algebra for All"** (California, late-1990s-2000s) pushed universal 8th-grade algebra and produced **mixed-to-negative** results — for the least-prepared students, taking algebra in 8th grade without adequate preparation was found "unambiguously harmful" (course repetition, no benefit): the **misplacement / under-prepared-acceleration** problem (Loveless, Brookings). At the other pole, **San Francisco's detracking** (2014-2024) **delayed algebra to 9th grade for everyone**, banning 8th-grade Algebra I explicitly to reduce racial tracking gaps. The **Huffaker, Novicoff & Dee (Stanford, 2023)** evaluation found the reform **did not narrow racial gaps**: white/Asian precalculus enrollment stayed more than double Black/Hispanic, Black/Hispanic calculus stayed under 10% before and after, and affluent families bought private acceleration — leading the board to **restore 8th-grade Algebra I in 2024** (phased back by 2026). The **2023 California Mathematics Framework** carries the live version of this fight, promoting a **data-science pathway** and detracking — but UC's **BOARS** ruled Data Science does **not** substitute for Algebra II for admissions, undercutting the "equal alternative" premise.

**Evidence grade: the controversy is well-documented; the underlying efficacy claims on both sides are contested/weak.** The honest read for a leader: **both under-prepared forced acceleration AND blanket detracking failed in their largest real-world trials** — the evidence supports **placement accuracy plus support**, not a single tracking ideology. Present the tradeoff (equity/access vs. STEM-gateway preservation) and the BOARS constraint; do not endorse either camp's efficacy claims. (The general legal/entitlement dimension of placement routes to `education-law`; this chapter states the *practice* evidence.)

## 4. Use-cases for a K-12 administrator

Enumerated distinctly, not blurred:

1. **Referee a math-methods faculty fight** (standard algorithms vs. invented strategies, timed tests, "productive struggle"). Bring the convergence: explicit instruction wins for acquisition and struggling learners; guided/enhanced discovery (Alfieri d=+0.30) beats both pure discovery and pure telling; NMAP refuses either pole. The war is largely settled toward "both, sequenced by expertise."
2. **Set a fact-fluency policy** that survives the anxiety debate. Build automaticity (it frees working memory), use reasoning-strategy phases, and separate timed *practice/activities* (WWC-endorsed) from high-stakes timed *tests* (contested). Know that the strongest direct study (Maki 2024) found no anxiety effect, and that Boaler's central source is a practitioner piece — so lead with the distinction, not a ban.
3. **Choose and defend a core curriculum or intervention program.** Screen with EdReports/state reviews for alignment (sibling file), then check **efficacy separately** on WWC / Evidence for ESSA — and read the effect size, not just the tier. A "Strong" label on a +0.04 effect is real but not transformational.
4. **Build a math MTSS system.** Pick screeners and progress-monitoring tools off the **NCII charts** (not vendor claims), watch grade-specific validity limits (e.g. easyCBM grade-3 validity), and staff Tier 2/3 with the cleanest-evidence targeted interventions (Fuchs-lineage) where budget allows.
5. **Audit a struggling grade band for a coherence gap.** Use the **Coherence Map** and **Progressions** to trace where a standard's prerequisites sit in earlier grades, and the **Focus by Grade** documents to check the building is teaching the major work, not spreading a mile wide.
6. **Diagnose and support suspected dyscalculia.** Know it is ~3-7%, comparable to dyslexia but massively under-identified, that the teacher-training gap is the bottleneck, and that CRA + systematic instruction (WWC PG#26, all-Strong) is the intervention core.
7. **Address math anxiety at the building and family level.** The highest-leverage move is parent-facing: math-anxious parents transmit anxiety *through frequent homework help* (Maloney 2015), so redesign homework and give parents scripts; deploy brief expressive-writing/reappraisal routines before high-stakes math.
8. **Make an algebra-placement / tracking decision.** The evidence indicts both under-prepared forced acceleration and blanket detracking; aim for **placement accuracy plus support**, know the Adelman gatekeeper finding, and — in California — the BOARS ruling that data science does not substitute for Algebra II.
9. **Vet a "research-based" math-program vendor claim.** Ask which ESSA tier, from which study, with what n, how many sites, and whether the effect size is an intent-to-treat average or a dosage-conditioned figure (the DreamBox +0.29 lesson).
10. **Design curriculum-based math PD** (e.g. CGI): expect real-but-modest, grade-dependent, fidelity-sensitive effects (g up to ~0.16) — the effect lives in implementation quality, so budget for sustained coaching, not a one-day workshop.
11. **Choose and govern manipulatives.** Prefer plain over perceptually rich, insist on explicit symbol-linking and **concreteness fading**, and expect benefits only with sustained use — manipulatives are not automatically helpful.
12. **Communicate a K-12 math philosophy to a board or community** that neither side can caricature: fluency *and* understanding are iteratively interdependent (the dichotomy is false), coherence and focus beat "mile-wide" coverage, and the SMPs name the reasoning goals that basic-skills scores never capture.
